The base model you pick decides your hardware bill, your license obligations, and your ceiling on quality — and you can't undo that choice cheaply once training starts. Before touching a training script, narrow the field on four axes: size, license, architecture, and how well it already does your task.

## Size: match parameters to your GPU, not your ambition

Bigger models generalize better before fine-tuning, but every extra billion parameters costs you VRAM, training time, and inference latency later. A 7-8B model fine-tunes comfortably on a single consumer GPU with [LoRA or QLoRA](/learn/fine-tuning/lora-and-qlora-fine-tuning) and serves cheaply. A 70B+ model needs multi-GPU infrastructure for training and serving, and only pays for itself when the task genuinely needs the extra reasoning capacity — long multi-step logic, broad world knowledge, nuanced writing.

Start smaller than you think you need. Fine-tuning narrows a model's behavior toward your task, so a well-tuned 8B model on a focused task often beats a generic 70B model prompted zero-shot. If evaluation later shows the small model plateauing below your bar, that's the signal to move up a size tier — not a guess made in advance.

## License: read it before you train, not after you ship

Open-weight doesn't mean unrestricted. Licenses fall into three rough buckets:

- **Permissive (Apache 2.0, MIT)** — free commercial use, no attribution requirements, no usage caps. Safest default for a product you intend to sell.
- **Custom "open" licenses** (Llama, Gemma-style) — free for most commercial use, but often carry a monthly-active-user threshold above which you need a separate commercial agreement, plus naming and acceptable-use clauses.
- **Research-only or non-commercial** — fine for internal tools and experimentation, a legal problem the moment you charge money for the output.

Check the license file in the model's repository directly, not a summary someone else wrote. Licenses change between model versions from the same family, and what applied to last year's release doesn't automatically apply to this year's.

## Architecture: dense vs. mixture-of-experts

Most base models you'll fine-tune are dense transformers — every parameter activates on every token, which makes training and quantization behavior predictable. [Mixture-of-experts](/learn/llm-foundations/mixture-of-experts-explained) models activate only a subset of parameters per token, giving you the quality of a larger model at closer to a smaller model's inference cost — but they're pickier to fine-tune well, since naive LoRA can under-train experts that rarely get routed to. Unless you have a specific reason to want an MoE's cost profile, a dense model is the safer default for a first fine-tuning project.

## Check the instruct version before you commit to the base

Most model families ship both a raw base checkpoint and an instruction-tuned variant. If your task looks like the model's existing chat behavior with a narrower domain or tone, fine-tuning the instruct version is usually faster and more stable than starting from raw base — you're adjusting behavior, not building it from scratch. Save true base-model fine-tuning for cases where you need to break out of the instruct model's habits entirely, such as teaching an unusual output format or a domain the instruct tuning actively fights against.

Whatever you pick, the choice isn't final — it's a hypothesis you test. Confirm it's the right one during [evaluation](/learn/fine-tuning/evaluating-a-fine-tuned-model), not before.
