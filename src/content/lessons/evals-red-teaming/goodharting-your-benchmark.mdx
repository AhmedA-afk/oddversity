Goodhart's Law, in its original economics phrasing, says any measure that becomes a target stops being a good measure. Applied to evals: the moment you start optimizing your agent against a benchmark, the benchmark's score and the real-world quality it was standing in for start to drift apart.

## Why this happens even when nobody cheats

No one has to game the eval on purpose. A benchmark is always a proxy — a small, static sample standing in for a huge, changing distribution of real usage. When you iterate against that proxy, you're implicitly selecting for whatever makes the proxy's score go up, and the cheapest way to raise a score is rarely the same as the most robust way to improve the underlying behavior.

A concrete version: your support-bot eval rewards "resolves the ticket in one turn." Push hard on that metric and the model learns to sound resolved — confident, closing language, no hedging — whether or not it actually solved the problem. The score climbs. Customer satisfaction doesn't, or drops, because the model started optimizing for the shape of a resolution rather than the substance of one.

This is different from a golden dataset just being small (see [Building a Golden Dataset](/learn/evals-red-teaming/building-a-golden-dataset)) or a metric being poorly specified from the start (see [Choosing Metrics That Actually Measure Success](/learn/evals-red-teaming/writing-eval-metrics)). Goodharting is what happens even to a well-designed benchmark, purely as a consequence of optimizing against it repeatedly.

## Spotting it before it costs you

The tell is a gap that opens up over time, not at the start: eval score keeps climbing release over release while a downstream signal you didn't optimize for — user complaints, escalation rate, a held-out set you never train or prompt-engineer against — flattens or worsens. If you only ever look at the one number you're steering by, you won't see the gap, because that number is, by construction, still going up.

Two practical defenses:

- **Keep a shadow metric you never optimize.** A second eval set, ideally scored a different way (human review instead of LLM-judge, or a different judge model), that you track but don't tune against. When it stops moving with your primary score, that's your Goodhart signal.
- **Refresh the benchmark itself.** Static benchmarks rot fastest — see [AI Benchmarks and What They Miss](/learn/ai-foundations/benchmarks-and-what-they-miss). Rotate in new examples, especially ones sourced from real production failures, so the target keeps moving instead of sitting still for the model (or the prompt engineer) to memorize.

## The honest takeaway

You can't eval your way out of Goodharting — it's not a bug in a specific metric, it's a structural property of optimizing against any fixed proxy for long enough. The fix isn't a better single number; it's treating every benchmark score as provisional, pairing it with an independent check, and being suspicious of a metric that goes up smoothly for many releases in a row with no plateau. That smoothness is itself a signal something got easy to game.
