Standard RAG trusts whatever the retriever hands back and generates from it, even if every chunk is irrelevant. Corrective RAG (CRAG) and Self-RAG fix this by making retrieval quality a checked step, not an assumption.

## The core idea: grade before you generate

After retrieval, insert a grading pass. A lightweight LLM call (or a small trained classifier) scores each chunk as relevant, ambiguous, or irrelevant to the query. That score decides what happens next, instead of every chunk going straight into the prompt.

CRAG, from the 2024 paper "Corrective Retrieval Augmented Generation," structures the outcomes into three branches:

- **Correct** — at least one chunk is clearly relevant. Keep it, optionally strip out noisy sentences within it, and generate normally.
- **Incorrect** — nothing retrieved is usable. Discard the set and fall back to a broader search, commonly a live web search, rather than forcing an answer from bad context.
- **Ambiguous** — mixed signal. Combine the usable pieces from retrieval with the fallback search results before generating.

This is a direct extension of [what RAG is for](/learn/rag/what-is-rag-and-when-to-use-it): grounding answers in real documents only works if the documents you retrieved are actually the right ones.

## Self-RAG: the model critiques itself at generation time

Self-RAG, from Asai et al. (2023), pushes the check further by training a model to emit special "reflection tokens" inline with its own output. Rather than a separate grader model bolted on afterward, the generator itself decides:

- whether to retrieve at all for this query
- whether each retrieved passage is relevant ("ISREL")
- whether its own draft answer is actually supported by that passage ("ISSUP")
- whether the answer is useful as a response ("ISUSE")

Because these judgments are learned during fine-tuning rather than prompted, Self-RAG needs a model trained specifically for it — you can't just ask an off-the-shelf model to emit calibrated reflection tokens and expect the same reliability. CRAG is the more practical starting point for most teams because it works as a wrapper around any retriever and any generator model, using prompted grading rather than a custom fine-tune.

## Why "abstain or re-retrieve" beats "always answer"

The failure mode both approaches target is the same one covered in [why models hallucinate](/learn/hallucinations/why-models-hallucinate): an LLM given irrelevant context doesn't reliably say "this doesn't answer the question" — it often blends the noise into a plausible-sounding but wrong answer. Grading retrieval before generation gives the pipeline an explicit off-ramp: re-retrieve with a rewritten query, expand to web search, or return "I don't have enough information" instead of guessing.

The tradeoff is latency and cost — grading adds at least one extra model call, and the corrective fallback path can add a full second retrieval round. That makes this pattern most worth adding where wrong answers are expensive: support bots grounded in a knowledge base, internal tools over policy documents, anything closer to [agentic RAG](/learn/rag/agentic-rag) where the system is expected to notice when it needs to look further rather than stop at the first retrieval.

## When to reach for this

Add a grading step once you've measured that irrelevant retrievals are actually reaching your generator — check this with the retrieval-precision metrics from [evaluating RAG quality](/learn/rag/evaluating-rag-quality) before adding the extra hop. If your retriever is already precise (tight metadata filters, a well-tuned reranker), the grading step mostly costs latency for little gain. It earns its place when queries are open-ended, the corpus is noisy, or an outdated-looking answer is worse than an honest "I don't know."
