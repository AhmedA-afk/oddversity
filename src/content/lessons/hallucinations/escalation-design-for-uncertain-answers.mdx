Teaching a model to say "I don't know" only helps if something happens after it says that. Escalation design is the plumbing: the threshold that decides an answer is too uncertain to ship, and the path that gets it to a human instead of silently returning a shaky guess.

## Pick a signal, not a vibe

You can't escalate on "the model seemed unsure." You need a measurable trigger. Common ones, roughly in order of how much infrastructure they cost:

- **Self-reported confidence.** Ask the model to rate its own certainty or explicitly output "insufficient information." Cheap, but self-reports are poorly calibrated — a model can be confidently wrong just as easily as honestly uncertain.
- **Retrieval coverage.** In a RAG system, if the retrieved chunks don't actually contain the answer (low similarity scores, empty results, or a check that the answer isn't grounded in the sources), that's a strong, cheap escalation signal — see [grounding](/learn/hallucinations/grounding-with-source-documents).
- **Sampling disagreement.** Run the same prompt two or three times, or at higher temperature, and compare answers. Divergent answers mean the model is guessing. This is the practical version of [semantic entropy](/learn/hallucinations/semantic-entropy-and-uncertainty-quantification), without needing a full entropy calculation.
- **A verifier or judge pass.** A second model call (or fact-checking step) scores the first answer's plausibility against sources or known constraints, and low scores route to escalation.

Combine two of these rather than trusting one. Self-reported confidence alone is the weakest single signal you can pick.

## Design the threshold as a business decision, not a model parameter

The confidence cutoff is not something you tune purely on an eval curve — it's a tradeoff between two costs: the cost of a wrong answer shipping, and the cost of a human reviewing something the model actually had right. A medical or legal summarizer should escalate aggressively, tolerating a lot of unnecessary human review. A recipe substitution suggestion can ship almost everything and escalate rarely. Set the threshold with the people who own that cost, and revisit it as you gather data — most teams start too conservative or too permissive and adjust after seeing real escalation volume.

## Build the handoff, not just the flag

A flagged-but-unrouted uncertain answer is worse than no flag at all, because it creates a queue nobody owns. The handoff needs: a place the escalated case lands (a review queue, a ticket, a Slack alert), a person or team with the context to actually resolve it, and a way the resolution gets back to the user without a dead-air delay. Also log what triggered the escalation — that data is what lets you retune the threshold later instead of guessing.

Escalation isn't a fallback bolted on when things fail. It's a deliberate second path your system routes to on purpose, and it should be tested as its own flow, not just left as a theoretical branch.
