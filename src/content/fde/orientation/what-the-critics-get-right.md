Every popular job title attracts a backlash, and most backlashes are noise. This one is not. The criticisms of Forward Deployed Engineering come from three groups who disagree with each other, and at least two of the three are describing something real. You should read them now, in week one, rather than in month seven.

There are four distinct criticisms. They are usually mashed together, which makes them easier to dismiss than they deserve.

## Criticism one: it is a rebranded solutions architect

This is the loudest, and it comes mostly from engineers outside the role.

A thread on Blind titled "forward deployed engineer is cancer of tech industry", started by a Netflix engineer, attacks the militaristic branding and argues the title reflects weak management thinking. Commenters call the role "solution architects", "customer engineers", and "IT consultants rebranded". On a Hacker News thread about an FDE explainer, the replies included "we were just called 'field engineers'", "'Professional services' was the other common term", "Forward Deployed Engineer is just a title change for Solutions Architects", and "military-branded marketing fluff".

Writers make the same argument at more length. Tom White's essay is titled "Sexy Titles, Unsexy Work" and its line is direct: the FDE is "for all intents and purposes, the old solutions engineer/technical consultant in a fresh uniform". Ben Piper's post title says it outright: the new job title for work consultants have always done. An AI architect writing under the name Wanjiko splits the title into three different jobs that share a name, embedded builder, rebranded sales engineer, and go-to-market role, and offers the sharpest single test in the whole literature:

> If a company has significantly more FDEs than product engineers, they're starting to look like a consultancy.

**What they get right.** Most of the tasks are not new. Field engineering, professional services and solutions architecture have existed for decades and involve embedding, integrating, and shipping in a customer's environment. A company can adopt the title without adopting anything else. When it does, the critics are describing that company accurately.

**Where the argument stops.** The insider rebuttal is narrow and specific: the difference is not the tasks, it is whether the field work is required to change the product. Gergely Orosz's summary of the distinction is that FDEs "contribute to core product development, whereas traditional Solutions Architects typically don't". First Round's guide for founders puts the test in one clause: the distinction "lies in whether those services feed back into product development".

An ex-Palantir FDE quoted in LeadDev gives the fairest version of the whole dispute: "The role is definitely real, but the title might have become a little bit frothy," with companies "calling an FDE anybody who's customer-facing".

Both things are true. The role is real. The title is being applied to jobs that are not it. Your task is to tell them apart, which is what the interview questions at the end of this page are for.

## Criticism two: the travel will burn you out

This one has the least argument attached to it because nobody disputes the facts.

Stated travel loads from real postings: OpenAI up to 50%, Scale's public-sector role more than 50% travel or relocation, Glean 25 to 50%, Cohere 20 to 40%, Shield AI around 30%, Anthropic around 25%, Databricks 20%. OpenAI's head of FDE puts on-site time at about 50%, rising to roughly 80% for engineers based in the UAE and Japan. Qureshi's phrase for his Palantir years is "a ton of travel", three to four days a week on site.

An ex-Palantir FDE told LeadDev that constant travel "in remote, hard-to-reach locations" makes the role susceptible to burnout earlier than others. Wikipedia's summary of the pushback notes that some engineers consider the role undesirable because of travel and "pressure to solve customer problems within relatively short amounts of time". A Blind thread on Palantir FDSE life describes "travel every other week" on some teams and centralised, low-travel work on others.

**What they get right.** Entirely. This is not a debate, it is a lifestyle fact, and it is not evenly distributed. It falls hardest on people with caregiving responsibilities, on people who need routine to stay healthy, and on anyone whose visa status makes frequent border crossing complicated.

**What to do with it.** Read the travel line before the compensation line. It is stated in 15 of 28 postings, and the ones that state it in days rather than percentages are the ones to look for: Vercel's "three to four days every other month" and Databricks India's "once every four to eight weeks" are recognisably different jobs from OpenAI's 50%.

## Criticism three: you may code less than a normal engineer

From a Blind thread on Palantir's FDSE role: the work is "jack-of-all-trades" with significant operations content, and "you may code less at this job than traditional SWE". An earlier thread describes the role as "basically a consultant to Palantir's clients. You build internal tools and analyze data", with projects that are either "interesting, varied" or "boring, monotonous" depending on the account.

The published accounts support the concern rather than refuting it. Cognition's deployed-engineering lead reports four to five hours a day on customer calls. One vendor survey of self-reported FDEs, caveated and directional, puts customer meetings at 31% of the week against 26% coding.

**What they get right.** If your goal is to become the deepest possible expert in one technical area, this is the wrong role. The 2022 Blind thread's own framing is fair: it is not ideal if you want a very specific expert-level engineering job at a large technology company, and it makes you valuable to startups that need well-rounded engineers.

**The counterweight.** Blind posters also note you can move between FDE and core engineering internally, and the founder-exit pattern is real and documented. Palantir alumni founded a large number of companies, Ramp reports that seven of its sixteen FDEs were previously founders, and Glean's posting says former technical founders are strongly encouraged. Whether that counterweight matters depends entirely on what you want in five years.

There is also a quieter version of this criticism worth naming. One 2026 guide estimates that around 80% of deployment work is legacy integration. That figure is unsourced and should not be quoted as data, but the direction is consistent with every first-person account in this path. Much of the job is unglamorous plumbing.

## Criticism four: the business model does not work

This is the operators' argument, it is the most technically serious, and it is the one candidates ignore.

Barry McCardel, who did the strategist job at Palantir for nearly five years, says the model requires Fortune-500 customers, genuine market uncertainty, and an extensible platform, a set of conditions he calls "exceptionally rare and very hard to pull off". It also requires leadership that will "not only accept the chaos, but embrace it" and let the field steer the roadmap, which he calls anathema to most cultures. He is candid about the cost at Palantir: "we were doing projects for free", with margins on some deployments "literally negative infinity".

Bob McGrew's advice to founders copying the model, per summaries of his talk, was essentially not to, because it is a trap that is dangerously easy to turn into a low-margin consulting firm.

Thomas Otter writes that venture capital is "fetishising the FDE" the way it fetishised product-led growth, and lands the sharpest formulation of the risk: "If the FDE is billable, they are working for the project, not the product," and "Building myopically at and for one customer is not a product, it's a project."

Ted Mabrey, Palantir's head of commercial, attacks the copiers rather than the critics, and his charge is that most imitators "are replicating the form but not the function", that they "simply redraw role boundaries and redistribute integration costs", and that they remain constrained by a business strategy that limits what they can sign up for.

First Round's guidance to founders says do not hire FDEs for product-led or freemium motions, rigid product visions, or a uniform ideal customer profile. PostHog says exhaust the standard routes to product-market fit first, because the model "only suits companies with high-margin products or strict regulatory environments".

An executive quoted in LeadDev makes the sustainability jab: many of these solutions exist "to remove human dependencies", and the companies selling them "are really adding expensive, hard-to-find humans to replace humans".

**Why this matters to you and not just to founders.** If the model does not work at the company you join, your job disappears or degrades. The team gets measured on billable delivery. The product feedback loop is dropped first because it is the least urgent thing on the list. Two years later you have a résumé that reads as consulting, which is exactly the outcome the critics predicted.

You are not just choosing a role. You are choosing a company where the role can survive.

## The two debates, separated

It is worth stating this plainly, because the noise comes from conflating them.

**Engineers argue about whether the title is honest.** Mostly it is a rebrand of field and professional-services engineering, with one real difference: a mandated product feedback loop.

**Operators argue about whether the economics work.** Mostly only upmarket, only with a platform to generalise into, and only if leadership will let the field steer the roadmap.

Neither group is claiming the work does not exist. Both are claiming that a lot of companies are doing it badly, and the postings support them: the same title now covers an embedded builder at Anthropic, an infrastructure engineer at Sierra, and a demo-and-pilot role at a platform vendor.

## Three questions that test a specific company

Since the criticisms are company-specific rather than role-specific, test the company.

1. **"What is the last thing the FDE team shipped that changed the core product?"** A real answer names a feature, a default, an SDK method or an eval set. "We give lots of feedback" means the loop is aspirational.
2. **"How many FDEs are there relative to product engineers?"** Use the consultancy test above. A team that is mostly FDEs, with no plan for that to change, is a services organisation.
3. **"Is FDE time billable to the customer?"** If yes, Otter's criticism applies directly: the incentive is to serve the project, not the product. OpenAI's stated approach is to avoid services revenue and keep the FDE team small and product-focused, pushing scale work into a separate organisation. That is one structural answer. There are others. "We haven't thought about it" is not one.

## Do this now

One journal entry. Which of the four criticisms worries you most, and what specific evidence would settle it for a company you are considering?

If the answer is travel, the evidence is a number in days, in writing, before you accept. If the answer is the business model, the evidence is the ratio and the billability. If the answer is coding less, the evidence is asking two current engineers on the team what they shipped last month. None of those questions is rude. All of them are questions a good FDE would ask a customer, which is part of why interviewers respect them.
