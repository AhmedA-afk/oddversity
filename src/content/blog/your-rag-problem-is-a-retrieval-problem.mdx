There is a predictable sequence when a retrieval system gives a bad answer. Someone rewrites the prompt. Someone suggests a larger model. Someone proposes a reranker, an agentic loop, a knowledge graph. Weeks pass. The answers stay wrong.

Almost always, the passage containing the answer was never in the prompt at all. No prompt can fix that, and no model can either.

## The five-minute diagnostic

Take five questions your system answers badly and that you know the corpus can answer. For each one, skip generation entirely and print the top ten retrieved chunks.

Then read them. Not the scores — the text.

You will land in one of two places, and they are different bugs with different fixes.

**The right passage is in there.** Then it is a generation or context problem: too many chunks diluting attention, the passage buried in the middle of a long context, or a prompt that does not require the model to use what it was given. Reranking, tighter top-k and a stricter grounding instruction all apply here.

**The right passage is not in there.** Then everything downstream is theatre. You cannot rerank a candidate set that lacks the answer, and asking a stronger model to reason over the wrong evidence produces a more articulate wrong answer.

In my experience the second case is far more common, and it is the one that teams spend the least time on — because retrieval is boring and prompts are fun.

## Why the passage went missing

Four reasons cover most of it.

**The chunk lost its meaning at the boundary.** A table separated from its headers. A step separated from its procedure. A clause separated from the definition of "it". The chunk retrieves fine — it just no longer means what it meant in the document. Read your actual chunks; the boundaries are usually visible as soon as you look at them, and almost nobody looks.

**The query and the document use different words.** The user asks about "returns"; the policy says "merchandise disposition". Dense embeddings handle paraphrase better than keyword search, but not arbitrarily far, and they are weakest exactly where domain jargon lives.

**The term was rare and exact.** Product codes, error strings, version numbers, surnames. Dense vectors compress; rare tokens are what compression discards first. This is the single strongest argument for hybrid search, and the failure is easy to reproduce: search for an identifier you know exists and watch it not come back.

**There were four near-identical chunks.** Duplicated boilerplate, or five versions of the same document, and your top-5 is now five copies of the same paragraph. Effective k is one. Deduplicate before you conclude anything about your k.

## What to do about it, in order

**Build a retrieval-only eval.** Twenty questions, each with the passage that should be retrieved. Score recall at k — did the right chunk appear in the top k at all. This number is more useful than any end-to-end answer score, because it isolates the half that is actually broken.

**Add hybrid search.** For most real corpora this is the largest single quality improvement available, and it is a smaller change than people expect: run a keyword search alongside the vector search and merge the rankings.

**Fix your chunk boundaries.** Split on the document's structure — headings, paragraphs, function definitions, table rows with their headers — and treat size as the fallback rather than the strategy.

**Then, and only then, rerank.** Reranking is a precision tool. It reorders what retrieval found; it cannot conjure what retrieval missed. Applied to a candidate set with 60% recall, an excellent reranker gives you an excellently ordered list that is wrong 40% of the time.

## The uncomfortable version

If your retrieval recall is 60%, your answer accuracy has a ceiling of 60% no matter what you do downstream. Every model upgrade, every prompt revision, every architectural flourish is redistributing quality inside that ceiling.

Measure the ceiling first. It's a morning's work, and it will tell you whether the last month of prompt iteration was ever going to help.
