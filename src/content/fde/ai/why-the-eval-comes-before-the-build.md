You have a demo. It runs on the six documents the customer emailed you, it produces a clean answer, and the room is impressed. Then the head of operations asks the only question that matters: "How do we know it is right?"

If you do not have an answer, the next four months are pilots, anecdotes, one loud user who found a bad output, and a slow slide into "let us revisit this next quarter". If you do have an answer, and the answer is a number the customer helped choose, the conversation moves to deployment.

That answer is the eval. Colin Jarvis, who runs OpenAI's Forward Deployed Engineering team, calls the practice **eval-driven development**, and lists "evaluation frameworks and labeled datasets" first among the things his FDEs actually build. Anthropic's Forward Deployed Engineer posting lists "evaluation frameworks" as a hard requirement alongside production application work. Across the job postings this path is built from, evals appear more often than RAG and far more often than prompt engineering. The distinguishing competence is not "can build a retrieval system". It is "can build the eval that proves the retrieval system is good enough for a regulator".

## What an eval actually is

Strip the word of its research-paper connotations. In the field, an eval has four parts and nothing else.

1. **A fixed set of examples.** Real inputs from the customer's world, stored in a file, under version control. Not generated by a model. Not a random sample of yesterday's traffic that changes each run.
2. **A label per example.** What a competent person in that role would consider the correct output. Produced by the customer's expert, not by you.
3. **A scorer.** Code that takes a model output and a label and returns a number or a pass/fail. Sometimes exact match. Sometimes a rule. Sometimes a second model, carefully constrained.
4. **A threshold someone signed up to.** "Ninety-two per cent on the routing decision, and zero cases where a rejected claim is auto-approved." Two numbers, one of them asymmetric, both agreed before you build.

If any of those four are missing, you have a test harness, not an eval. A test harness tells you the code ran. An eval tells you whether the system is fit for the job.

## Why the order matters

Building the eval first is not an engineering hygiene preference. It changes three things about the engagement.

**It converts an opinion into a specification.** Customers do not say "I need 92% accuracy". They say "it has to be right". Sitting with the domain expert and labelling twenty real examples is how "right" becomes visible. Half the time the expert disagrees with themselves across similar cases, and you have found the actual problem before writing a line of code: the policy is ambiguous, and no model can fix that.

**It makes the build falsifiable.** Once the eval exists, every prompt change, every retrieval tweak, every model swap is a measurement rather than a debate. You stop arguing about whether the new system prompt is better. You run it.

**It survives the thing you cannot control.** Providers deprecate models. Your customer will, at some point in the next year, be told to move off the model you shipped on, or to move to a self-hosted one for data-residency reasons. If you have an eval, that migration is an afternoon and a report. If you do not, it is a re-implementation with no way to tell whether you made things worse.

That third point is the one procurement understands. In a bank or a hospital, "we can prove this behaves the same after the change" is worth more than any accuracy figure.

## The engagement arc it belongs to

Jarvis describes the shape of a frontier-lab deployment as: deep domain embedding on site, pick a problem whose solution is worth far more than the work, build the core solution over roughly six to eight weeks, then run extended pilots and refine the evals for four months or more, then codify what generalises. On the Morgan Stanley research-access deployment, the technical pipeline took six to eight weeks and reaching regulated-use confidence took about four further months of pilots and eval rigour, after which he reports about 98% advisor adoption.

Read that timeline again. The building is the short part. The proving is the long part. If you start the proving on day one you are running the two in parallel. If you start it after the demo, you have added four months to the end of the project and you are doing it under scrutiny rather than in private.

## Four failure patterns you will see

**The vibe demo.** Six hand-picked inputs, all of which work. It converts a meeting and nothing else. The fix is to ask, on the first day, for twenty examples the customer expects to be hard.

**The generic benchmark.** Someone reports a public leaderboard score. No customer cares how a model does on a general-knowledge test. They care whether it reads their claim forms. Domain-specific metrics or nothing.

**The symmetric metric.** A single accuracy number on a task where the errors are not equal. In a claims workflow, wrongly sending a valid claim to human review costs a few minutes. Wrongly auto-approving a fraudulent one costs money and possibly a regulator's attention. One number hides that. Always carry at least one asymmetric, must-never-happen count alongside the headline figure.

**The eval written by the person who wrote the prompt.** You will unconsciously label the cases your system handles. Labels come from the customer's expert. Your job is to make labelling cheap enough that they will do it.

## The FDE angle

In the field, the eval is a negotiating instrument as much as an engineering one.

When a stakeholder says "it must never be wrong", the eval is how you say no with an alternative. You put the labelled set on the screen and ask which of these twenty the system is allowed to get wrong. They will point at three. You have just been given a threshold and, usually, a human-in-the-loop step for the risky class.

When the scope grows, the eval is the boundary. A new intent means new labelled examples. No labels, no intent, no schedule change. That is a much easier conversation than "that is out of scope".

When you hand over, the eval is the handover. A runbook rots. A test suite someone can run on a Friday afternoon and get a report from does not. Leaving an eval harness behind is one of the concrete things Harvey and Cohere list as FDE deliverables in their own postings, and it is the artifact that lets the customer keep the system alive without you. Making yourself unnecessary is the point.

## What you should be able to do now

An interviewer can test this directly and often does. Given "we want an assistant that answers questions about our HR policies", a weak answer starts describing chunking. A strong one asks who decides whether an answer is correct, requests twenty real questions with the answers that person considers right, and says what the two numbers are going to be before touching a model.

Write your eval charter now. One page, and reuse it for every build in this path:

- The decision the system makes, in one sentence, in the customer's vocabulary.
- Who the expert is, by name and role.
- Where the examples come from, and how many.
- The primary metric, and the threshold.
- The must-never-happen event, and its allowed count (usually zero).
- Which slices you will report separately (language, channel, branch, document type).
- Who runs the harness after you leave.

The next three lessons fill in the rows: getting the twenty labels, running the feasibility test before you promise anything, and choosing metrics a customer will actually accept.
