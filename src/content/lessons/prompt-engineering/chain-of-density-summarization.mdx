Ask a model for "a dense summary" and you usually get something vague or something bloated. Chain-of-density sidesteps both by making density a process, not an instruction: generate a summary, then rewrite it several times at the same length, each pass required to fold in new information the last one missed.

## The core loop

The technique (from the 2023 "From Sparse to Dense" paper on GPT-4 summarization) runs in fixed iterations, usually four or five:

1. Write an initial summary of the source text — deliberately generic, at a fixed length (say, 80 words).
2. Identify 1-3 entities from the source that are missing from the summary — specific names, numbers, or claims, not vague topics.
3. Rewrite the summary at the *same length*, fusing in those entities. Something has to go to make room.
4. Repeat steps 2-3 for a set number of rounds.

The length cap is what makes this work. Without it, a model just appends detail until the "summary" is as long as the source. With it, every new fact displaces a filler phrase or a redundant clause, and by round four or five what's left is almost pure signal.

## Why not just ask for one dense pass

A single-shot request for a "dense, information-rich summary" gives the model no way to know what "denser" means relative to its own first attempt — it has nothing to compare against, so it defaults to its normal register: readable, moderately generic, safely padded. Chain-of-density gives it an artifact to interrogate each round. "What's missing from *this specific draft*" is a concrete, checkable task; "be dense" is not. It's the same reason [chain-of-thought prompting](/learn/prompt-engineering/chain-of-thought-prompting) outperforms "think carefully" — a process beats an adjective.

## A worked shape

Round 0 (generic): "The article discusses a company's product launch and its market reception."

Round 2, after pulling in two missing entities: "Acme's Q3 launch of its analytics tool drew 40,000 signups in the first week, beating internal projections by 3x."

Round 4, after more rounds of the same length: "Acme's analytics tool launched Sept 12, hit 40,000 signups in week one (3x internal projections), and pushed the stock up 8%."

Same word budget throughout, strictly more claims per sentence. Notice each round is also more compressed syntactically — dense summaries lean on appositives and parentheticals because full clauses are too expensive.

## Where this breaks

Push past 4-5 rounds and density crosses into unreadability — summaries become entity lists wearing sentence punctuation, which is what the original paper flags as the human-preference ceiling: raters preferred round-3-ish outputs over the maximally dense final round. Density is also not the same as faithfulness — a model under length pressure to add entities can quietly invent one to fit the space, so treat this as a compression technique that still needs the checks you'd run against [summarization hallucination](/learn/hallucinations/summarization-hallucination). And it costs 4-5x the tokens of a single summary call, so it's worth it for a pull-quote or an abstract, not for every summary in a high-volume pipeline.
