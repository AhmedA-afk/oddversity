[Hallucination Evaluation and Benchmarks](/learn/hallucinations/hallucination-evaluation-and-benchmarks) makes the case that you need a real eval, not spot-checking, and that public benchmarks are a starting filter rather than a substitute for your own. This is the deferred rigor behind that claim: what four widely-cited benchmarks actually measure, mechanism by mechanism, and exactly where each one's blind spot sits. Treat it as optional depth — skip it if you just needed to know benchmarks aren't enough; read it if you need to pick one, or explain to a skeptical teammate why a leaderboard number didn't predict your production behavior.

## TruthfulQA: imitative falsehoods

TruthfulQA's questions are adversarially written so that the statistically likely continuation — the one that echoes a common misconception, myth, or folk belief present in training text — is false. It's not testing whether a model knows obscure facts; it's testing whether a model will confidently repeat a popular wrong answer when the popular wrong answer is exactly what fluent text would predict comes next. Scoring runs on two axes at once: truthfulness and informativeness, deliberately paired so that a model can't win by refusing to answer everything — a model that says "I have no comment" to every question scores perfectly truthful and close to zero informative, which the paired score is built to catch. That two-axis design is itself a small instance of the [abstention-quality metric](/learn/hallucinations/what-to-measure-metrics) from earlier in this module, baked directly into a benchmark's scoring rule.

**Blind spot:** it's a fixed, well-known, widely mirrored question set. A model's training data or fine-tuning process can absorb the specific "correct, contrarian" answers to these specific questions without the model generalizing the underlying skill of resisting imitative falsehood on questions it's never seen phrased this way. The question style also skews toward trivia-adjacent, culturally specific folk beliefs — not the shape of question your internal support bot or coding assistant actually gets.

## HaluEval: can a model tell hallucinated from correct

HaluEval pairs correct and hallucinated versions of the same QA, dialogue, and summarization samples, where the hallucinated variant was generated by prompting another LLM to produce a plausible-sounding but wrong alternative. The task it measures is discrimination: given both versions, can a model or detector correctly identify which one is the hallucination? That's a meaningfully different question from "how often does this model hallucinate when generating freely" — HaluEval measures recognition, not generation-time behavior.

**Blind spot:** the hallucinated samples were themselves produced by a specific generating model with a specific fabrication style. A detector tuned against HaluEval learns to recognize *that* style of hallucination well — and can miss the idiosyncratic ways your own production system fails, which rarely match the style of whatever model authored the benchmark's negative examples. This is the same concern [Evaluating the Detector Itself](/learn/hallucinations/evaluating-your-detector) treats formally: a detector's measured recall is only as good as how representative its training and test hallucinations are of what it'll actually face.

## FActScore: atomic-fact precision for long-form text

Built for long-form generation — its original use case was model-written biographies — FActScore decomposes a passage into atomic facts (independent, minimal factual units), checks each one against a trusted knowledge source, and reports the fraction supported. It is a precision metric: it tells you what share of what was actually stated holds up. It does not check recall — it has nothing to say about facts that should have been included but weren't, so it never penalizes incompleteness, only unsupported content that made it into the output. [The implementation lesson](/learn/hallucinations/factscore-eval-impl) builds exactly this scorer end to end.

**Blind spot:** because it only scores stated claims, a model that hedges into near-total vagueness — writing three uncontroversial, easily-verified sentences instead of a detailed twelve-sentence biography — can post a high FActScore precision while delivering far less useful content than a more detailed, occasionally-wrong answer. High precision and high usefulness are not the same thing, and FActScore alone can't tell them apart. Decomposition quality also matters more than it first appears: an LLM asked to split text into atomic facts can merge two claims into one or split one claim into several, shifting the count either way before verification even starts.

## SimpleQA: short factual QA with a knows-what-it-knows angle

SimpleQA uses short, closed-form, fact-seeking questions that have a single, uncontested, verifiable answer, and grades responses into three buckets — correct, incorrect, and not-attempted — rather than folding refusals into "incorrect." That third bucket is the point: it's explicitly built to reward honest abstention over confident guessing, which makes it one of the few widely cited benchmarks that scores calibration directly rather than as an afterthought.

**Blind spot:** the question format — short, closed, single crisp answer — is close to the opposite shape from what most production systems actually generate: multi-paragraph, multi-source, RAG-grounded explanations where faithfulness to retrieved context matters more than recall of a single trivia fact. A strong SimpleQA score says very little about whether a system stays faithful across a long, sourced answer, and faithfulness under retrieval needs its own metric family entirely — see [What to Measure](/learn/hallucinations/what-to-measure-metrics) for why factual accuracy and faithfulness are scored separately in the first place.

## A model can win one and lose another

Consider two hypothetical assistant configurations tuned differently. One is tuned to hedge readily and flag uncertainty — it does well on TruthfulQA (rewarded for not repeating confident myths) and on SimpleQA's not-attempted credit, but when asked to write a detailed biography for a FActScore-style eval, it produces so few concrete, checkable claims that the "supported fraction" is high only because there's almost nothing to check — informative it is not. The other configuration is tuned to sound confident and specific — it states many atomic facts per answer, which tanks its FActScore precision the moment a few of those specifics are wrong, and it readily repeats plausible folk beliefs on TruthfulQA — yet it might score fine on a HaluEval-style discrimination task if its particular fabrication style doesn't resemble whatever model generated that benchmark's negative examples. Neither configuration is well described by any single one of these four scores. That's not a flaw in the benchmarks — it's a direct consequence of each one measuring a genuinely different mechanism, exactly as [What to Measure](/learn/hallucinations/what-to-measure-metrics) predicts.

## Why the number rarely transfers to your domain

None of these four benchmarks were built on your documents, your users' phrasing, or your risk tiers. Treat a public benchmark score as a coarse, floor-level filter for model selection — the same caution [AI Benchmarks and What They Miss](/learn/ai-foundations/benchmarks-and-what-they-miss) makes generally about benchmarks in AI — never as evidence about your specific hallucination rate. The only number that answers that question is one computed on your own domain, which is exactly what [Building a Golden Hallucination Eval Set](/learn/hallucinations/building-golden-eval-set) walks through next.
