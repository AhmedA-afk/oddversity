Imagine a student who memorizes every answer on last year's practice exam word-for-word, but bombs the real test because the questions are phrased differently. That student learned the *answers*, not the *material*. Machine learning models can fail the exact same way, and the name for it is overfitting.

## What generalization actually means

A model is trained on a set of examples so it can perform well on examples it has never seen. That ability to handle new, unseen input is generalization — it's the entire point of training a model in the first place. Nobody needs a model that only works on data already sitting in the training set; you could just look up the answer directly.

Generalization works when the model learns the underlying pattern connecting inputs to outputs. If you train a model to recognize cats, generalization means it learns "whiskers, pointed ears, fur texture, general shape" — features that hold up across photos it's never encountered.

## What overfitting looks like

Overfitting is what happens when a model, instead of learning that pattern, learns the noise and idiosyncrasies specific to its training examples. Given enough capacity and not enough constraint, a model can essentially memorize: "this exact pixel arrangement equals cat," "this exact sentence equals positive sentiment." It scores close to perfect on the data it trained on and falls apart on anything new.

The telltale sign is a gap between training performance and performance on held-out data. A model scoring 99% on its training set but 60% on fresh examples isn't a good model — it's a model that crammed for the wrong exam. This is why practitioners always split data into a training set and a separate held-out (validation or test) set: training accuracy alone tells you almost nothing about whether the model actually generalized.

## Why it happens

Overfitting tends to show up when a model has more capacity to memorize than the training data has genuine signal to teach it, or when there just isn't enough training data to make memorization impractical. A model with millions of parameters trained on a few hundred examples has plenty of room to memorize every one of them rather than being forced to find the general rule.

The opposite failure, underfitting, is a model too simple or too undertrained to capture the pattern at all — it does poorly on both training and new data. Generalization sits in the middle: enough capacity and training to capture the real signal, not so much that it starts capturing noise instead.

## Why this matters for working with LLMs

Large language models are pretrained on enormous, varied datasets specifically because scale and diversity push models toward generalization rather than memorization — see [The Data the Model Learned From](/learn/ai-foundations/the-data-the-model-learned-from). It's also why [AI Benchmarks](/learn/ai-foundations/benchmarks-and-what-they-miss) can mislead: a model that has effectively "seen" a benchmark's questions during training can look like it generalizes when it's actually just recalling.
