A network makes a prediction, compares it to the right answer, and gets a single error number. Backpropagation is how that one number turns into thousands of tiny "nudge this weight up, nudge that one down" instructions — one for every weight in the network.

## The problem it solves

Forward pass: input goes in, flows through layers, a prediction comes out, and you compute a loss — how wrong that prediction was. But the loss doesn't tell you which weight to blame. A weight three layers deep contributed to the output only indirectly, filtered through every layer after it. You need to know, for each weight, "if I increase this by a tiny amount, does the loss go up or down, and by how much?" That sensitivity is the weight's gradient, and computing all of them naively — perturbing one weight at a time and rerunning the whole network — would be brutally slow for a model with billions of weights.

## The chain rule, applied layer by layer

Backpropagation gets every gradient in roughly the same time as one forward pass, by reusing work. It walks backward from the loss, one layer at a time, applying the [chain rule](/learn/maths-foundations/gradients-and-slopes) from calculus: the effect of a weight on the loss is the effect of that weight on its layer's output, multiplied by the effect of that output on everything downstream.

Concretely, each layer receives a gradient describing "how much did the loss change per unit change in my output" and does two things with it:

```text
layer.backward(gradient_from_above):
    grad_wrt_my_weights = gradient_from_above * (my output's sensitivity to my weights)
    grad_wrt_my_input   = gradient_from_above * (my output's sensitivity to my input)
    return grad_wrt_my_input   # passed to the layer before me
```

The first line is what you actually wanted — the gradient for this layer's weights, which a training step will use to nudge them. The second line is what makes the whole thing efficient: it hands the next layer back exactly the information it needs, already combined with everything downstream. No layer ever needs to know about layers more than one step away.

## Why "assigning blame" is the right mental picture

Each layer only ever asks a local question — "given how much the layer after me says it wants to change, how should my inputs and weights change?" — and passes that answer backward. Repeat that from the output to the input, and every weight ends up with a precise share of responsibility for the final error, even weights nowhere near the output. This is also why the technique needs the network's operations to be differentiable: every "how much did output shift when input shifted" step depends on being able to compute a slope.

Once every weight has a gradient, [gradient descent](/learn/maths-foundations/gradient-descent-intuition) is what actually uses it — subtracting a small multiple of the gradient from each weight, and repeating over millions of examples.
