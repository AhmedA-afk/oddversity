*This is a deep-dive on one placement decision introduced in [The Residual Stream and Layer Norm](/learn/llm-foundations/residual-stream-and-layer-norm) — where exactly `norm()` sits relative to the `+`. It's optional depth; the practical takeaway is short: modern LLMs default to pre-norm, almost always with RMSNorm specifically.*

Two ways to wrap a sublayer in a residual connection look almost identical on paper, and behave completely differently at depth.

<PreNormPostNorm />

## The two placements

```
post-norm:  x = LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x))
pre-norm:   x = x + Sublayer(LayerNorm(x))
```

Post-norm is what the original "Attention Is All You Need" transformer used: run the sublayer, add the residual, *then* normalize the sum. Pre-norm normalizes the input on its way *into* the sublayer, and adds the raw, un-normalized result straight onto the untouched residual stream.

## The gradient-flow argument, precisely

Take the gradient of the output with respect to the stream several layers back, and trace what each placement forces it to pass through.

**In post-norm**, the residual path itself is inside the normalization: the quantity that gets passed to the next layer is `LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x))`, so *every single layer*, the gradient flowing backward through the `+` still has to flow through that layer's `LayerNorm`'s Jacobian before it reaches the previous layer. LayerNorm's Jacobian isn't the identity — it depends on the input's own mean and variance, and its magnitude isn't guaranteed to stay near 1 across every layer. Multiply a gradient through dozens of these non-identity Jacobians in sequence, and the same vanishing/exploding risk that residual connections were introduced to solve creeps back in, just one level removed from where it started. In practice this is exactly why deep post-norm transformers are reported as sensitive to the learning-rate warmup schedule: without a slow ramp-up, the accumulated effect of many non-identity norm Jacobians early in training tends to destabilize the network before it settles into a good region.

**In pre-norm**, the residual path is `x = x + Sublayer(LayerNorm(x))` — notice that the `x` being added is the *raw* stream value, untouched by any normalization. `LayerNorm` only ever appears inside the branch feeding the sublayer, never on the path the `+` operates on directly. Backpropagating through an addition copies the gradient unchanged to both branches, so the gradient reaching the residual stream at any earlier layer is the *direct* gradient from the final loss, with zero normalization Jacobians multiplied in along that specific path — a genuinely clean "gradient highway," end to end, from the last layer's output back to the embeddings. This is the property that lets pre-norm transformers train stably at far greater depth, typically with a much gentler warmup requirement than post-norm needs at the same depth.

## The tradeoff pre-norm doesn't erase

Pre-norm isn't strictly better in every respect, and stating that precisely matters: because the residual path is never rescaled, nothing stops the *magnitude* of the stream itself from growing steadily with depth — each layer's un-normalized contribution keeps landing on top of an already-large running sum. Very deep pre-norm models can end up with activation norms that grow roughly with depth, which shows up as a practical wrinkle: outputs from very early layers and very late layers can differ enormously in scale, which is part of why a final `LayerNorm` (or `RMSNorm`) is still applied once, right before the unembedding step, regardless of which placement a model uses throughout its blocks — the stream needs rescaling at the exit even though it flowed unrescaled all the way through. Post-norm doesn't have this particular growth issue, because every layer's output gets renormalized on the spot — its cost is purely in how much harder that renormalization makes early training.

## RMSNorm: what gets dropped

LayerNorm computes two statistics per token vector — the mean, which it subtracts, and the variance, which it divides by — before applying a learned scale `gamma` and shift `beta`:

```
LayerNorm(x) = ((x - mean(x)) / sqrt(var(x) + eps)) * gamma + beta
```

RMSNorm drops the mean-centering step entirely and rescales only by the root-mean-square of the vector's components:

```
RMSNorm(x) = (x / sqrt(mean(x^2) + eps)) * gamma
```

There's no `beta` shift term in the standard formulation either — RMSNorm only rescales magnitude, it never re-centers or shifts the vector's direction. The justification for dropping mean-centering is empirical and specific: across the models it's been tested on, most of LayerNorm's stabilizing benefit comes from controlling the *scale* of activations, not from forcing their mean to exactly zero, so removing the mean computation (and the `beta` parameter that went with it) measurably cuts compute — one fewer statistic to compute, one fewer parameter tensor — for a quality difference reported as negligible relative to standard LayerNorm.

## Why modern LLMs default to pre-RMSNorm

Put the two results together and the pairing that dominates recent open model families (LLaMA, Mistral, and others) follows directly: pre-norm because the gradient-highway argument makes stacking many dozens of layers reliable, and RMSNorm specifically (rather than full LayerNorm) in that pre-norm slot because it delivers most of the same stabilization for less compute, applied at every one of those many layers where the saved computation adds up. Neither piece is a law of nature — some architectures still use post-norm variants, or hybrid placements, and research into other normalization schemes continues — but "pre-RMSNorm" is the specific combination you should expect to see as the default when you open a recent model's config, which [Reading a Real Model Config](/learn/llm-foundations/reading-a-real-model-config) shows you how to actually go find.

## Where this leaves you

Post-norm and pre-norm look like a one-line difference in where parentheses go. The consequence of that one line is the difference between a residual stream that stays clean of any normalization end to end, and one that gets re-normalized at every layer — and that difference is what training stability at real depth actually hinges on.
