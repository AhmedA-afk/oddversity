Ask a search engine for "the best-rated dentist near me" and it hands you links to real pages you can click and check. Ask a plain AI chatbot the same question and it can hand you an equally confident, fluently formatted list of dentists — names, addresses, star ratings — that simply don't exist, because it isn't looking anything up. It's predicting what an answer like that usually looks like.

## Search finds. Chat generates.

A search engine is a retrieval system: it has already crawled and indexed real pages, and when you query it, it returns pointers to documents that exist independently of your question. You can open one, see who wrote it, see when, and judge it yourself. That's the whole contract — see /learn/ai-literacy/ai-is-not-a-search-engine for the full mechanism.

A plain chatbot is a generation system. It has no index of current pages and no retrieval step by default — it produces the next most statistically plausible chunk of text given your prompt and its training, one token at a time. Nothing forces that text to correspond to anything real. It might, often does, and for reasoning or drafting tasks that doesn't matter. But for a factual lookup, "plausible-sounding" and "true" are two different properties, and a plain model only guarantees the first one.

That gap is where almost every beginner mistake with AI comes from. Here are six real, well-documented versions of it — the mechanism behind each, how to spot it happening to you, and the concrete fix.

## "What's the best-rated dentist near me?"

### The mistake
You ask a general-purpose chatbot with no live web access a location- and time-sensitive question — best dentist, restaurant open now, cheapest flight today — and expect a directory-quality answer.

**Why it's wrong:** the model has no database of local businesses, phone numbers, or current ratings. It has read enormous amounts of text that *looks like* business listings and reviews, and it reproduces that shape: a numbered list, a name, an address-shaped string, a star rating. Producing the correct format is easy for it. Binding that format to a real, currently-operating business at that address is a completely different problem it has no mechanism to solve.

**Symptom:** you get a clean top-5 list that reads exactly like a real search result. Then you call the number and it's disconnected or belongs to someone else, the address is a parking lot or a different kind of business entirely, and you can't find the quoted reviews anywhere. Every entry has a similar too-smooth cadence ("known for their gentle approach and modern equipment") because it was written to sound like a listing, not copied from one.

**Fix:** anything tied to place and time — near me, open now, in stock, currently rated — goes to an actual search engine or maps app, full stop. If you want to use AI for this, use a tool that performs a live search and shows you its sources, and then click through and confirm the top result actually exists before you act on it.

## Trusting the sources it just made up

### The mistake
You ask the AI to back up a claim with citations, links, or case law, and you accept them because they're formatted correctly — author, year, title, sometimes even a URL or DOI-shaped string.

**Why it's wrong:** a citation is a very learnable shape. The model has seen millions of examples of "Author (Year). *Title.* Journal, Volume(Issue)." Generating something in that shape is a much easier prediction task than retrieving one specific real document from a verified index — which a plain chatbot has no way to do. So it produces a string that looks exactly like a citation with no actual link between that string and any real paper, case, or article.

**Symptom:** the link 404s, the DOI resolves to nothing, the case can't be found in any legal database — or worse, the source is real but doesn't say what the AI claimed it says.

This is not a hypothetical. In a real 2023 U.S. court case (*Mata v. Avianca*), attorneys submitted a legal brief containing case citations generated by ChatGPT. Several of the cited cases did not exist. Opposing counsel and the judge couldn't locate them because they had never existed, and the court sanctioned the attorneys once this came to light. It was widely reported at the time and remains one of the clearest documented examples of exactly this failure mode, from people who should have known to check.

**Fix:** treat every AI-generated citation as a lead, not a source. Search the exact title or case name yourself. If you can't independently find and open the underlying document, it isn't verified — see /learn/ai-literacy/how-to-verify-facts-and-sources for the process.

## Asking it for today's news, scores, or prices

### The mistake
You ask "who won last night's game," "what's the stock at right now," or "what's the latest on X" in a plain chat window and trust whatever comes back as current.

**Why it's wrong:** a model's knowledge comes from a training dataset with a fixed cutoff date, and without a connected tool it has no channel to the present moment (see /learn/ai-literacy/where-ai-knowledge-comes-from-and-stops). Crucially, it doesn't experience "not knowing" the way you do. It will still generate the most statistically plausible continuation of your question — which can mean restating an old result as if it were current, or producing a guess shaped exactly like a real answer.

**Symptom:** the score, date, or figure is stale or simply wrong, sometimes off by a whole season or news cycle. Sometimes the model does caveat with "my knowledge has a cutoff" — but not reliably enough to count on. Silence about the limitation is not the same as the limitation not applying.

**Fix:** for anything time-sensitive, go to a search engine directly, or use an AI product explicitly built for live retrieval that shows its sources so you can check when the underlying page was published. Don't rely on a plain chat window's self-assessment of what it currently knows.

## Treating a quote or number as gospel

### The mistake
You ask for an exact quote from a person, book, or speech, or a specific figure — a date, a population count, a dollar amount — and use the wording or number exactly as given.

**Why it's wrong:** generating text is reconstructive, not retrieval. For very famous lines the model may reproduce them closely, but it has no internal flag distinguishing "I am quoting this verbatim" from "I am producing text that is stylistically consistent with what this source would say." The same applies to numbers: a model predicts a plausible-looking digit sequence token by token; it isn't looking a value up in a table, so figures get blended, transposed, or gently invented without any signal that anything went wrong.

**Symptom:** the quote is almost right — one word swapped, two sentences merged, attributed to the wrong person or the wrong work entirely. The number is suspiciously round, or it just doesn't match when you check the primary source.

**Fix:** for a quote, search the exact phrase in quotation marks and confirm it appears in the original text or transcript. For a number, ask yourself where it would actually come from, then go check that source directly — treat the AI's figure as a placeholder for the real one, not the real one itself.

## Confusing fluency with accuracy

### The mistake
You judge how much to trust an answer by how confident, detailed, and well-formatted it sounds — clean bullet points, specific-looking numbers, a decisive tone — rather than by whether you've actually checked it.

**Why it's wrong:** fluency is precisely what a language model is built to produce. It is optimized to generate the statistically likely next piece of text given everything before it — not to be correct. That process reliably produces smooth, confident, specific-sounding prose whether or not the content underneath is accurate. There's no internal confidence meter that tracks truth the way careful hedging does for a person who actually knows the limits of their own knowledge. See /learn/ai-literacy/why-ai-sounds-so-confident for why this happens by design, not by accident.

**Symptom:** you catch yourself thinking "it wouldn't be this specific if it were wrong." An exact street address, an exact percentage, a named sub-clause — these feel like evidence of accuracy, but a model can produce exactly that level of specific detail while fabricating as easily as while reporting something real. The surface confidence doesn't move.

**Fix:** hold "sounds authoritative" and "is verified" as two completely separate questions. Before acting on any claim you can't already vouch for, ask: could I open an independent source right now and confirm this specific detail? If not, it's unverified regardless of how it reads. This is the single habit worth building — see /learn/ai-literacy/the-single-most-important-skill-judging-output.

## Staying in chat when you should switch tools

### The mistake
You notice the task is really a lookup — current price, real address, latest software version, who currently holds a role — and keep working in the same plain-chat conversation instead of switching to a tool built for that job.

**Why it's wrong:** different AI products have different plumbing under the hood. A plain chatbot is just trained weights: no live access, no retrieval step. A search-grounded assistant (browsing mode, a tool with web search enabled) adds an actual retrieval step — it queries the web at the moment you ask and generates its answer anchored to what it retrieved, usually showing you the pages. That's a different pipeline, not just a different "mode" of the same trust level — see /learn/ai-literacy/matching-the-ai-tool-to-the-job.

**Symptom:** you re-ask the same chatbot "are you sure?" two or three times, hoping repetition produces a correction, instead of just going and looking. Or the answer you got just "feels old" and you keep working with it anyway because switching tools feels like extra friction.

**Fix:** before you ask, classify the task in one second. Reasoning, drafting, explaining, brainstorming — a plain chatbot is the right tool. A current fact, a price, a local result, "who is X right now" — that's a search engine or an AI tool with live web access and visible sources, every time, no exceptions.

## Pre-flight checklist

Run this before you trust or act on anything an AI told you as fact:

- [ ] Is this tied to a specific place, time, price, or "right now"? If yes, don't ask a plain chatbot — use search or a tool with live, visible sources.
- [ ] Did it give you a citation, link, name, or case? Open it yourself before you repeat it anywhere that matters.
- [ ] Would you bet money the exact number or quote is verbatim correct? If not, go find the primary source before using it.
- [ ] Are you trusting this because it sounds confident and detailed, or because you've actually checked it? Tone is not evidence.
- [ ] Is the task reasoning and drafting (AI's strength) or retrieval of a current fact (search's strength)? Match the tool to the job, not the other way around.
- [ ] What does it cost if this is wrong? Higher stakes always earn more verification, no matter how good the answer sounded.
