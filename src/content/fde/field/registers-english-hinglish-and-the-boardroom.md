Register is not just vocabulary level. It is the whole bundle: which language, how formal, how direct, how much is said out loud versus left for a follow-up email. An FDE who can only operate in one register — polished, standard-English, conference-room English — will read every room that does not match it as somehow less professional, and that misreading costs trust in exactly the rooms where the real information lives.

This is the same underlying skill covered in [Executive communication: the pyramid, then the detail](/roles/forward-deployed-engineer/field/executive-communication-the-pyramid): the same content, delivered in the form the audience actually uses. Register is the spoken, in-the-room version of that discipline.

## Code-switching in an Indian business room is not informality

In a large number of Indian workplaces — a co-operative bank's back office, a manufacturing plant's shop floor, a hospital's nursing station — colleagues move between English and Hindi, or English and another regional language, inside a single sentence. This is not a sign the room is unprofessional or that English is not understood. It is often the fastest, most precise way for two people who share a first language to say something to each other, with English supplying the technical vocabulary that arrived with the software, the compliance term, or the job title, and the rest of the sentence carrying the emotional weight.

A branch officer describing yesterday's failed case might say something like: *"Yeh jo aapka model hai na, woh kal wale case pe kaam nahi kiya."* ("This model of yours — it didn't work on yesterday's case.") The English word is "model", because that is the word the vendor introduced. Everything around it, including the slightly weary "hai na" that softens a direct complaint into something more like a shared observation, is doing communicative work that a flat English sentence would lose. Missing that this is a direct complaint, because it arrived wrapped in a mixed-language sentence, is the actual risk here — not the mixing itself.

**What this means practically:**

- **Do not correct it or comment on it.** Treating code-switching as something to apologise for ("sorry, should we speak in English?") signals that you find the room's normal way of talking to be a problem, which is a worse read than any of the language mixing itself.
- **Do not perform it if you are not a fluent speaker.** Dropping in a Hindi word you learned for the occasion, to seem approachable, usually reads as exactly what it is. Native speakers set the code-switch; you follow their lead by staying comfortably in English until the room shows you something else, and by not over-interpreting warmth as an invitation to switch languages yourself.
- **Listen for what gets said in the shared language that would not get said in English.** A direct complaint, a joke at the vendor's expense, a "just do the needful" that means "I don't have the authority to specify exactly what I want, please use judgement" — these often surface more honestly outside the formal-English register. If you only understand the English half of the room, ask afterwards, one on one, rather than in the moment: "what was the concern about the review queue — I want to make sure I got that right."
- **A side conversation in another language during a technical discussion is not necessarily exclusion.** It is frequently two people confirming a detail faster than doing it in English for your benefit would allow. If it happens often and you are missing something you need, ask directly and specifically: "was there a concern about the timeline just now — I don't want to miss it."

## Written deliverables hold a different register than the room

Whatever mix the room speaks in, the SOW, the decision memo, and the status update are written in formal, standard English regardless — these are documents that get forwarded to people who were not in the room, sometimes outside the country, and a mixed register in a written deliverable reads as unprofessional in a way it does not when spoken. The skill is not "match the room in everything". It is knowing that spoken register and written register are two different decisions, made separately.

## US rooms: efficient, blunt is not rude

A US stakeholder meeting, especially at a startup or in a technical review, tends to reward getting to the number fast and treats a direct disagreement as normal working communication rather than a breach of politeness. "That won't work, here's why" said early in the meeting is not read as confrontational; a long, careful lead-up to the same point is more likely to be read as evasive, or as not having done the analysis. "Let's take this offline" is meant literally — it means end this thread now, not later in this same meeting.

## Northern European rooms: procedural, direct is the default, and written confirmation matters more than verbal reassurance

The Halden Logistics data protection officer from [the discovery lab](/roles/forward-deployed-engineer/field/discovery-lab-interview-the-simulated-customer) is a useful model here: precise, unhurried, and willing to say "I cannot sign off on that" without softening it, because in that register a direct refusal is a normal, respectful answer, not a harsh one. What moves her is not warmth, it is specificity and a documented answer — she has been told "we anonymise it" before by a vendor who could not explain how, and it was wrong. A verbal assurance carries less weight in this register than it might elsewhere; the follow-up email with the actual method, in writing, is what earns trust.

## The pattern underneath all three

None of this is a fixed national character, and treating it as one is the mistake to avoid — individuals vary more than any regional pattern, and the honest move when you are unsure is to ask rather than assume: "should this update go out in English only, or is it fine to include the Hindi I heard in the room?" is a real, useful question, not an awkward one. What is consistent across all three settings is the underlying discipline: notice what register the room is actually using, do not treat a register different from your default as a deficiency in the room, and keep your written record in the formal register regardless of how the meeting itself was spoken. That combination — flexible in the room, consistent on paper — is what lets the same person work a Meridian branch office and a Halden Logistics compliance review in the same month without either one reading as the wrong version of you.
