Preparation for an FDE loop usually focuses on the technical and case-study rounds. This page covers the part of the process that a strong technical performance does not protect you from: a company's location and visa decision, which can be made late, separately from the interview panel, and can override everything else.

## What happened

The account documented in the module on startup loops is worth returning to in full here. A candidate went through a five-stage ElevenLabs FDE loop: a recruiter screen that pressed hard on individual ownership, a timed online assessment, live coding in a shared document, a case study built around an airline CTO scenario, and a final CTO round. They performed well enough to receive a verbal yes.

The offer was then rescinded. The candidate's own account of why: "they decided they only want to hire in SF... they saw some immigration risks." This happened despite the candidate being willing to relocate. Nothing about the candidate's technical performance is reported to have changed between the verbal yes and the reversal; the decision came from a different part of the company, later, and overrode the loop's own outcome.

The candidate's own retrospective advice, stated plainly in their write-up, is to "force clarity on" location and visa status early in the process, rather than letting it surface only once an offer is on the table. That is the single actionable lesson from this case, and it is worth taking literally: ask early, not because it guarantees a better outcome, but because it means a rejection on those grounds costs you a recruiter screen instead of a completed loop and a verbal offer.

## What "we do sponsor visas" actually commits a company to

Anthropic's own FDE posting states "we do sponsor visas" but does not guarantee sponsorship for every role or every candidate. Read that distinction carefully, because it is easy to read past it. A blanket "we sponsor visas" statement on a careers page describes the company's general policy, not a commitment to your specific application. Sponsorship decisions for a given role can still turn on headcount location requirements, the specific visa category available, immigration risk assessments similar to the one in the ElevenLabs case, or budget for legal costs that were never disclosed to you during the loop. Treat a general sponsorship statement as evidence the door is not closed, not as a promise it is open for you.

This is not unique to Anthropic; it is standard language across companies that sponsor at all, and the ElevenLabs case shows exactly how a general willingness can still end in a specific reversal.

## The questions to ask, and when

Ask these in the recruiter screen, the first stage of almost every loop covered in this module, not after a verbal offer:

1. **"Is this role open to candidates who will need visa sponsorship, and has the team sponsored a visa for this specific role before?"** A recruiter who has not been asked this before may need to check; that is fine, but get the answer in writing before your next round, not verbally in a call you cannot point back to.
2. **"Is there a specific office location requirement for this role, and is remote or hybrid genuinely open, or is that flexible only until an offer stage?"** The ElevenLabs case is precisely a scenario where remote or relocation appeared open through the loop and was closed at the end.
3. **"What is the realistic timeline for a visa decision relative to a start date?"** Sponsorship processes, where they exist, often run on a timeline the interview loop does not accommodate; knowing this early lets you plan rather than discover it under a deadline.

Asking these questions does not disqualify you or signal a lack of confidence. A company that treats a direct, early question about visa and location as a red flag is telling you something useful about how they would have handled the actual complexity of hiring you.

It also helps to ask them in writing, not only on a call. A recruiter's verbal answer in a screen is easy to walk back later, as the ElevenLabs case shows happened even after a verbal yes on the offer itself. A follow-up email that restates what was said, "just to confirm what we discussed, this role is open to candidates needing visa sponsorship and the location is flexible for the right candidate," gives you something to point back to if the answer changes later, and it costs you nothing to send.

## What this means if you are applying from India

If you are applying to a US-based FDE role from India, read the above as a floor, not a ceiling, of due diligence. Every frontier-lab posting verified in this path lists a US office location with stated travel expectations, not a remote-from-India arrangement, so any conversation about visa sponsorship for a role like this is, by definition, also a conversation about relocation. Ask both questions together: is sponsorship realistic for this role, and is the company actually prepared to relocate someone from where you are, not just from within the US. The ElevenLabs candidate was already based in a location the company could plausibly have hired locally instead, and the offer still fell through on immigration risk; treat that as the honest baseline of how this can go, not as an unusual worst case.

None of this is a reason to avoid applying. It is a reason to ask the two or three questions above before you spend three or four weeks of loop preparation on a role that was never going to clear its own internal sponsorship bar.
