## Common mistakes

Two people can use the exact same AI tool and both end up worse off — one because they trusted it completely, the other because they trusted it not at all. Here's what each failure actually costs, in real cases, and the boring middle ground that avoids both.

## The over-truster: three ways blind faith gets expensive

The over-truster treats AI output the way you'd treat a signed report from a colleague who's never been wrong before. The problem is that no such colleague exists, and AI isn't one either — it's fluent, not accountable, and fluency is not the same signal as accuracy. See [why AI sounds so confident](/learn/ai-literacy/why-ai-sounds-so-confident) for the mechanism behind that fluency.

### Mistake: filing AI-written citations without opening a single case file

In 2023, a New York personal-injury lawyer named Steven Schwartz used ChatGPT to research case law for a federal filing in *Mata v. Avianca*. ChatGPT invented six court cases whole-cloth — plausible names, plausible docket numbers, plausible quotes, none of them real. Schwartz never opened a case reporter to check. Opposing counsel couldn't find the cases. The judge, P. Kevin Castel, couldn't find them either. He sanctioned Schwartz, his co-counsel, and their firm, and it became one of the most widely reported AI failures in any profession — and it has since repeated, in other courtrooms, with other lawyers, more than once.

**Why it's wrong:** a language model generates the most statistically plausible next words, not a lookup against a legal database. A fake case citation is generated by exactly the same process as a real one — the model has no internal flag that distinguishes "this happened" from "this is the shape of something that happens." Fluency and correctness are produced by the same mechanism, so fluency tells you nothing about correctness on its own. See [what a hallucination really is](/learn/ai-literacy/what-a-hallucination-really-is).

**Symptom:** the output is specific, confident, and formatted exactly like the real thing — real-sounding names, real-sounding numbers, no hedging. That specificity is precisely what makes it convincing and precisely what makes it dangerous, because specificity feels like evidence.

**Fix:** for anything with real consequences — a legal filing, a financial figure, a claim about a person — verify against the primary source before it leaves your hands, every time, no exceptions for how confident the answer sounded. For citations specifically, that means opening the actual case, not just checking that a source is named. See [how to verify facts and sources](/learn/ai-literacy/how-to-verify-facts-and-sources).

### Mistake: letting a customer-facing bot's promises stand as company policy

In 2024, a grieving passenger asked Air Canada's website chatbot about bereavement fares. The bot told him he could apply for a discount retroactively after booking. That wasn't Air Canada's actual policy. When the airline refused to honor it, the passenger took them to British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal — and Air Canada actually argued that the chatbot was "a separate legal entity" responsible for its own words. The tribunal rejected that outright: the company put the tool in front of customers, so the company owns what it says. Air Canada had to make good on the promise.

**Why it's wrong:** deploying an AI system to represent you doesn't transfer responsibility to the AI — it just moves the point where a human should have checked its work earlier in the process, before it ever reached a customer. Nobody had audited what the bot would say about an edge case like retroactive bereavement fares, so nobody caught it until a customer did.

**Symptom:** the failure surfaces downstream, at the worst possible time — in front of a customer, a regulator, or a court — rather than during testing, because nobody stress-tested the tricky questions before launch.

**Fix:** before you let an AI system speak for you (a support bot, an auto-generated policy summary, a drafted email your name goes on), treat every sentence it can produce as something you're on the hook for, same as if an employee said it. Test it against your actual edge cases first, not just the happy path. See [when AI helps and when it hurts](/learn/ai-literacy/when-ai-helps-and-when-it-hurts).

### Mistake: mistaking a plain, confident tone for a checked fact

When Google rolled out AI-generated summaries at the top of search results in 2024, one summary told a user to add non-toxic glue to pizza sauce to help the cheese stick. It traced back to an eleven-year-old joke comment on Reddit that the system had surfaced as if it were settled advice — stated flatly, no hedge, no "this appears to be satire." Several similarly confident, similarly wrong summaries went viral the same week, each one presented with the identical calm authority as the summaries that were actually correct.

**Why it's wrong:** the model's phrasing doesn't change based on how good its source was. A joke, a stray forum post, and a peer-reviewed paper all get compressed into the same even, declarative tone — the system isn't grading its own sources as it writes, it's predicting fluent-sounding text. Confidence in the prose is not confidence in the claim.

**Symptom:** you can't tell the reliable answers from the wrong ones by reading them, because they read identically. The tell isn't in the output — it's in whether you check.

**Fix:** when an AI answer states something specific or surprising, ask where it could plausibly have come from, and go look. Don't let smooth phrasing substitute for a source you can point to. See [catch a hallucination: worked example](/learn/ai-literacy/catch-a-hallucination-worked-example).

## The dismisser: two ways reflexive refusal gets expensive

The dismisser is the mirror image: one bad answer, and the tool is dead to them — even for the tasks it was never wrong about. This isn't stubbornness for its own sake; it's a predictable, well-studied response, and it has its own real cost: you quietly lose access to something that would have made you faster, for tasks it's genuinely fine at.

### Mistake: quitting a net-useful tool after one visible miss

Researchers Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons, and Cade Massey ran a now-well-known study on what they called "algorithm aversion." People watched a forecasting algorithm make predictions — it was more accurate than human forecasters on average — and then watched it make a mistake. Afterward, given a real choice of which forecaster to rely on, people who'd seen the algorithm err even once became reluctant to use it again, markedly more reluctant than they were toward a human forecaster who'd made a similar mistake. The algorithm was still the better bet. People picked the worse one anyway, because the algorithm's single miss stuck with them more than its steady, unremarkable string of correct calls ever did.

**Why it's wrong:** this is a bias in how humans weigh evidence, not a fact about the tool. A single vivid failure is more memorable and more emotionally charged than a long run of quiet successes, so it gets overweighted when you decide what to trust next time — even when the honest tally still favors the tool.

**Symptom:** you can name the one time it burned you in specific, vivid detail, but you can't recall any of the times it was fine — not because it wasn't fine, but because "fine" doesn't leave a memory.

**Fix:** before writing a tool off, ask what the realistic alternative actually is — your own unaided attempt, most likely — and how *its* error rate compares, honestly, not against a hypothetical perfect standard. Keep a running sense of where AI has actually helped or hurt you by task type, not a single global verdict from one incident. See [the real limits of AI today](/learn/ai-literacy/the-real-limits-of-ai-today) and [matching the AI tool to the job](/learn/ai-literacy/matching-the-ai-tool-to-the-job).

### Mistake: generalizing one failure mode to "AI is useless"

This is the pattern behind most "I tried AI once and it was wrong, so I don't use it" stories: someone asks it for exact arithmetic on a long number, or an obscure citation, or today's date, gets a wrong answer, and concludes the whole category of tool can't be trusted — including for drafting an email, summarizing a document, or explaining a concept, tasks where it's actually reliable. The failure was real. The generalization from it wasn't.

**Why it's wrong:** "wrong at task A" and "wrong at task B" aren't the same claim just because the same tool produced both. Language models are specifically unreliable at precise computation and exact recall of obscure facts — [when AI gets numbers and math wrong](/learn/ai-literacy/when-ai-gets-numbers-and-math-wrong) explains why — but that limitation doesn't transfer to tasks that don't require exact recall, like rephrasing, structuring, or brainstorming.

**Symptom:** you stop using AI for the tasks it's actually good for (a first draft, a summary, an explanation of unfamiliar code) because of a bad experience with a task it was never suited for in the first place.

**Fix:** when AI gets something wrong, narrow the conclusion to that specific capability, not the whole tool. Ask "was this the kind of thing it's known to be bad at?" before writing off the rest. See [what AI can and can't do](/learn/ai-literacy/what-ai-can-and-cant-do-overview) and [should I use AI for this — worked decisions](/learn/ai-literacy/should-i-use-ai-for-this-worked-decisions).

## The balanced middle: a capable assistant you always supervise

Both failure modes come from the same wrong mental model: treating AI as something you either fully hand the keys to or fully lock out. The working model is closer to [delegating to a sharp but eager intern](/learn/ai-literacy/prompting-is-delegating-to-an-eager-intern) — someone genuinely capable, widely read, fast, and tireless, who will also confidently hand you a wrong answer with zero hesitation and no personal stake in being caught. You wouldn't let that intern file a legal brief unsupervised, and you also wouldn't fire them after one mistake and start doing all the research yourself by hand.

In practice, calibrated trust looks like:

- **Match the checking to the stakes.** A low-stakes draft gets a skim. A number that goes in front of a client, a court, or a customer gets independently verified before it leaves your hands. See [the verification checklist](/learn/ai-literacy/the-verification-checklist).
- **Decide what "checked" means before you ask.** If you can't say in advance what evidence would confirm or contradict the answer, you're not actually planning to verify it — you're planning to trust it and call that verifying.
- **Track reliability by task, not by tool.** "Good at summarizing my meeting notes, bad at exact dates" is a usable, specific memory. "Bad" or "good," full stop, is not — it throws away exactly the information you need next time. See [uncertainty and verification](/learn/ai-literacy/uncertainty-and-verification).

## Pre-flight checklist

Before you let AI output go somewhere that matters:

- [ ] Would getting this wrong be expensive, embarrassing, or hard to walk back?
- [ ] Have I checked the specific facts, numbers, or citations against a real source — not just skimmed for plausibility?
- [ ] If this represents me or my organization to someone else, have I tested it against the tricky cases, not just the easy ones?

Before you write a tool off after a bad answer:

- [ ] Was this a task type it's specifically known to struggle with (exact math, obscure facts, very recent events), or a general failure?
- [ ] What's the realistic alternative — my own unaided work — and how does its error rate actually compare?
- [ ] Am I about to stop using it for tasks it never failed at, just because of this one?
