Every time you send a prompt, the model doesn't read your text — it reads a fixed-size buffer of tokens. Once that buffer is full, something has to give: older tokens fall out, or the request fails. That buffer is the context window, and its size is baked into the model, not chosen per request.

## Why the limit exists

The mechanism doing the reading is [attention](/learn/llm-foundations/attention-mechanism-explained): every token computes a relationship score against every other token in the window. Double the window, and the number of pairwise comparisons roughly quadruples, along with the memory needed to store them. That's why context windows didn't casually grow from a few thousand tokens to hundreds of thousands — it took real architectural work (sparse attention, better KV-cache management, algorithmic tricks that avoid the naive quadratic blowup) to make longer windows affordable to serve.

This is also why the window is symmetric — it's not "how much you can paste in," it's the combined size of everything the model reads and writes for that turn: your prompt, any system instructions, retrieved documents, prior conversation turns fed back in, and the reply it's about to generate. A conversation that grows across many turns is quietly re-consuming window space on every single call, because chat isn't stateful on the model's side — the full history gets re-sent each time.

## What happens when you hit the ceiling

Context windows don't degrade gracefully by default. Depending on the interface, you'll see one of a few behaviors: the API rejects the request outright, or the client silently truncates or drops older turns to make room for new ones. Neither is "the model getting worse" in the way people often describe it — it's tokens physically falling outside the window before the model ever sees them.

There's a separate, subtler failure mode worth knowing about: even well inside the limit, models don't attend to all positions equally. Performance on information buried in the middle of a very long context tends to be worse than on information near the start or end — an effect sometimes called "lost in the middle." A generous window doesn't guarantee the model actually uses all of it well; see [context rot](/learn/context-engineering/context-rot) for more on how quality degrades as you fill the window, independent of whether you've technically exceeded it.

## Working with a fixed budget

Because the window is fixed and shared across input and output, context management is a real engineering problem, not an afterthought. Three practical levers show up constantly: only put in what's actually relevant instead of dumping full documents ([retrieval vs. stuffing](/learn/context-engineering/retrieval-vs-context-stuffing)), explicitly track how many tokens each part of your prompt costs ([token budgeting](/learn/context-engineering/token-budgeting-strategies)), and compress old conversation turns into summaries once they're no longer central ([summarization for compaction](/learn/context-engineering/summarization-for-compaction)).

None of this is optional at scale. A support bot with a long-running conversation, or an agent chaining dozens of tool calls, will hit the ceiling if nothing manages what stays in the window — and once you understand that the limit is architectural, not a product dial someone forgot to increase, it's obvious why every serious LLM application needs a strategy for it.
