A "multimodal" model isn't a text model with an image plugin bolted on. It's a single transformer where pixels, sound, and words all get converted into the same kind of object — a vector — before anything else happens.

## Everything becomes a token

Text tokenization is familiar: a byte-pair encoder chops "hello world" into a handful of subword pieces, and each piece maps to an embedding vector, as covered in [Tokenization: How Text Becomes Tokens](/learn/llm-foundations/tokenization-explained). Images go through a parallel process. A vision encoder — usually a small transformer of its own, following the ViT (Vision Transformer) approach — slices an image into a grid of patches, say 16x16 pixels each, and turns every patch into a vector. A 512x512 image might become a few hundred "image tokens." Audio gets a similar treatment: a spectrogram or raw waveform is chunked into short time windows, each encoded into a vector.

The trick is that all of these vectors land in the *same* embedding space as text tokens, discussed in [Embeddings: Turning Tokens Into Geometry](/learn/llm-foundations/what-are-embeddings). A patch of an image and the word "cat" can end up near each other geometrically if the model has learned that association. Once everything is a sequence of vectors, the transformer doesn't know or care which ones came from pixels and which came from letters — it just runs [attention](/learn/llm-foundations/attention-mechanism-explained) over the whole sequence.

## Why this works: shared architecture, joint training

This unification is what makes multimodal models different from older pipelines that bolted a separate image-captioning model onto a language model. In a modern multimodal LLM, the image encoder and the language decoder are trained together (or the image encoder is trained to align with a frozen language model's embedding space), so the model learns to reason across modalities rather than translate one into the other and lose information in the handoff.

This is also why context windows quietly shrink when you paste in images. Those image patches count as tokens against the same [context window](/learn/llm-foundations/context-window-mechanics) as your text — a single high-resolution image can easily cost more tokens than a page of prose.

## Output is a different story

Understanding images and audio is one capability; generating them is another. Most "multimodal" chat models are multimodal on the *input* side (they read images/audio/video) but still only produce text tokens as output, using [next-token prediction](/learn/llm-foundations/next-token-prediction) over a text vocabulary. True any-to-any models that generate images or audio typically add a separate decoder — a diffusion model or an audio vocoder — downstream of the shared transformer, rather than predicting pixels one at a time the way they predict text.

## What this unlocks in practice

Because everything shares one embedding space, a multimodal model can be asked to compare a photo against a paragraph of text, describe a chart, transcribe and summarize a voice memo, or answer a question about a specific frame of video — all without a separate specialized model per modality. The tradeoff is cost and latency: encoding a video's worth of frames into tokens is expensive, which is why most multimodal products sample frames sparsely rather than feeding in every one.
