An LLM API call is slow and expensive compared to a normal database query, which means the failure modes are different too: a traffic spike doesn't just slow your app down, it can burn through your monthly budget in an hour or trip your provider's account-wide rate limit and take every user down at once. Rate limiting and backpressure are how you put a ceiling on that.

## Two different limits, two different jobs

There are really two things to protect, and they need separate limits. The first is your upstream provider's rate limit — usually expressed as requests per minute and tokens per minute, shared across your whole account. Blow through it and you get `429` errors for every user, not just the one who caused the spike. The second is your own budget and fairness policy — you don't want one user or one script consuming the capacity everyone else needs. Track both: an account-level limiter that never lets your total outbound rate approach the provider's ceiling, and a per-user (or per-API-key) limiter that caps any single caller well below that.

A token bucket is the usual mechanism for both: each user (or the account as a whole) has a bucket that refills at a steady rate and drains by request count or token count. It's forgiving of short bursts, unlike a hard "N requests per minute" window that resets abruptly and lets everyone burst at the boundary.

```python
if not bucket.take(tokens=estimated_tokens):
    return queue_or_reject(request)
```

## Backpressure: what happens when the limit is hit

Rejecting the request outright is the simplest response, but often the wrong one — a user who gets a hard error for sending one message too quickly just retries immediately, which makes the spike worse. Backpressure means pushing back on the caller instead of dropping them: a queue that holds the request briefly and processes it once capacity frees up, or a `429` response that carries a `Retry-After` header so a well-behaved client backs off instead of hammering you. This pairs directly with the [retry strategy](/learn/genai-app-dev/rate-limits-and-retry-strategies) on the calling side — a limiter without a documented retry contract just moves the failure from your server to the client's error logs.

Queuing has a limit of its own: don't let a queue grow unbounded while users stare at a spinner. Cap the queue depth and the max wait, and once either is exceeded, reject with a clear message rather than let latency creep past what anyone will wait for.

## Degrade instead of failing outright

The most resilient systems don't just accept-or-reject at the limiter — they have a cheaper fallback to degrade to. That can mean routing overflow traffic to a smaller, faster model instead of queuing it behind the primary one, serving a cached or approximate answer for read-heavy features (see [prompt and semantic caching](/learn/production/prompt-and-semantic-caching)), or temporarily disabling a non-critical AI feature via a [kill switch](/learn/production/feature-flags-and-kill-switches) while keeping the core product usable. Decide which of your AI features are essential and which can be shed under load *before* the incident, not during it — that decision belongs in the same [on-call playbook](/learn/production/on-call-playbooks-for-ai) that covers the rest of your failure response.

## What to watch

Rate limiting only works if you can see it working. Track rejection and queue rates per user and per feature, and alert well before you're regularly hitting the ceiling — a limiter that's constantly saturated is a signal to raise a quota or add capacity, not just a safety net doing its job quietly forever.
