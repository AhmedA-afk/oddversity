The previous three pages walked through what specific loops look like. This one collects what the people running those loops, and the people who have hired for the role for years, say they are actually screening for. Read it as a checklist against yourself, not as trivia about the industry.

## Decompose before you solve

Exponent's aggregated guide, built from candidate reports across multiple companies, lists "jumping to a solution in the decomposition round" as the number-two rejection pattern industry-wide, and calls decomposition "the single most important filter in the FDE process." The same guide flags three smaller but recurring tells: saying "we" instead of "I" when describing your own work, giving a generic motivation answer instead of a specific one, and going silent during technical rounds instead of narrating your thinking. All four are fixable with practice; none of them require more technical skill than you already have.

## Hustle over credentials

Joe Schmidt at a16z, writing the essay that named this "the hottest job in startups," put it plainly: "this kind of implementation work doesn't require a PhD, but it does require hustle." He describes the profile his firm looks for as "curious hustlers" with "a healthy disregard for the status quo," and notes that industry exposure helps but is not essential. This lines up with what the postings show elsewhere in this path: a degree is a hard requirement in only a minority of FDE listings, and a founder or consulting background substitutes for a title everywhere.

## Soft skills are the strongest separator, not a nice-to-have

Jeff Darnton, SVP of platform experience at Harper, told LeadDev that "soft skills and the ability to speak to customers are the strongest separator," because an FDE has to talk fluently to both engineers and CTOs in the same week, sometimes the same day. Piotr Kraus, an ex-Palantir FDE now at Platypus Technologies, adds a specific version of that skill: FDEs interrogate whether a proposed solution actually works and propose alternatives backed by data, rather than agreeing with whatever the customer suggested first. Kraus's own read on the title is candid: "the role is definitely real, but the title might have become a little bit frothy," a useful reminder that the substance interviewers are testing for is not the same thing as the hype around the title.

## The product loop, tested as a question

Ritika Singh of DataGOL, quoted in Deep Engineering's piece on how FDE hiring goes wrong, gives the cleanest one-line test in any of the research for this path: "shipping without extracting the pattern makes you a very expensive contractor. Extracting patterns without shipping makes you an analyst." Expect interviewers, especially at project deep-dive or portfolio-review stages, to probe for both halves: did you actually ship into someone's real environment, and did you write down what should change about the product because of it.

## Willingness to tell the customer they are wrong

Perspective AI's prep guide frames this as the quality that separates a genuinely good FDE from someone who is merely charismatic in the room. Treat this claim as one prep site's framing rather than a verified fact about hiring criteria, but it matches the pattern above: Kraus's "interrogate whether it actually works" and Singh's "extract the pattern" both require being willing to disagree with the person paying the bill.

## Both failure modes get screened out

The same Perspective AI guide names the two shapes of candidate that strong loops are built to catch and reject, at opposite ends of the same spectrum: the technically brilliant engineer who "freezes in a room full of skeptical customer stakeholders," and the "polished, articulate consultant who interviews beautifully but cannot actually write and ship the code." A loop that pairs a live coding round with a customer-simulation or case-study round, which every company covered in this module does in some form, is built specifically to catch whichever failure mode you have.

## What the people running these teams say, directly

Pragmatic Engineer interviewed three FDE leaders together, Colin Jarvis of OpenAI, Leo Mehr of Ramp, and Anjor Kanekar, who spent seven years as a Palantir FDE. Their common asks: strong software-engineering fundamentals, the ability to work alongside a sales team without becoming a salesperson, genuine comfort with ambiguity, and customer empathy. OpenAI draws a specific, useful line in that same piece between an FDE and a Solutions Architect: FDEs "write code directly on customer infrastructure," while Solutions Architects rarely do. If you are unsure whether a given company's "FDE" posting is really an FDE role or a relabelled SA role, this is the question to ask in your own recruiter screen.

Vin Vashishta, who has hired FDEs for several years and analysed roughly a hundred postings, names six competencies worth treating as a checklist:

1. **Technical breadth.** "FDEs ship real code into customer environments, not slides or demos."
2. **Customer-facing communication.** The ability to offer solutions in language a non-technical stakeholder understands.
3. **Problem decomposition.**
4. **End-to-end ownership.** In his words, "the job is over when the customer renews, not when the demo works."
5. **The product-feedback loop.**
6. **Domain and enterprise fluency**, including regulatory literacy such as SOC 2, HIPAA, and FedRAMP.

## The frame underneath all of it

Bob McGrew, formerly at both Palantir and OpenAI, described the role on YC's Lightcone podcast as a model of "doing things that don't scale, at scale." That is the tension every one of the loops above is designed to probe: can you do the unglamorous, specific, one-customer work well enough that a pattern falls out of it, without either refusing to do the work or losing yourself in it forever.
