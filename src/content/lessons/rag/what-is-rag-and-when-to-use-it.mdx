Ask a model a question about your company's internal handbook and it will confidently make something up — not because it's broken, but because it has never seen that document. RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) fixes this by fetching the relevant text first, then handing it to the model as context before asking for an answer.

## The pattern

RAG has three steps that repeat on every query:

1. **Retrieve** — search a knowledge base (usually a vector database of your documents) for chunks relevant to the user's question.
2. **Augment** — insert those chunks into the prompt, alongside the question.
3. **Generate** — let the model answer using only what it was just given, instead of relying on what it memorized during training.

The model isn't learning your data. It's reading it, fresh, every single time — the way you'd hand a colleague the right page of a manual instead of expecting them to have memorized the whole thing.

## The problem it solves

Two failure modes push teams toward RAG:

- **Hallucination on unknown facts.** A base model has no way to say "I don't know your refund policy" — it will pattern-match to something plausible-sounding instead. Grounding it in real retrieved text gives it something true to paraphrase from.
- **Stale knowledge.** Training data has a cutoff. Your product docs, support tickets, and internal wikis change weekly. Retrieval pulls from a live index, so the model always sees this week's version, not the one baked into its weights.

RAG doesn't make the model smarter. It makes the model's answers traceable to a source you control, which is also why RAG answers can cite where they came from.

## When RAG is the right call

Reach for RAG when the knowledge is large, changes often, or is private — a support knowledge base, internal documentation, a legal contract archive, a codebase. Retrieval scales to millions of documents without retraining anything, and updating the index is as simple as adding a new file.

## When to skip it

RAG adds real complexity: a chunking strategy, an embedding model, a vector store, and a retrieval step that can itself go wrong (see [Evaluating RAG Quality](/learn/rag/evaluating-rag-quality)). It's not free, so skip it when:

- The relevant material comfortably fits in a single prompt — just paste it in.
- The task depends on reasoning or style, not facts you own — fine-tuning or better prompting fits better.
- The knowledge is small and static enough to memorize once and rarely touch again.

If you do go the RAG route, the two decisions that matter most early on are how you split documents into chunks and how you turn those chunks into searchable vectors — covered next in [Chunking Strategies for Documents](/learn/rag/chunking-strategies-for-documents) and [Embeddings and Semantic Similarity](/learn/rag/embeddings-and-semantic-similarity).
