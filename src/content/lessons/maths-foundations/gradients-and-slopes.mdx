A slope tells you how a line rises or falls. A gradient does the same job for a function with many inputs at once — it's a vector that says, at this exact point, which direction increases the output fastest, and by how much.

## From one slope to many

Picture a hiker on a hillside described by height `= f(x, y)`, where `x` and `y` are position. At any spot, the ground isn't equally steep in every direction — it's steep going north, gentle going east, flat along some diagonal. The gradient collects the slope in each individual direction (each partial derivative) into a single vector: `∇f = (∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y)`.

That vector has two properties worth remembering:

- **Direction**: it points toward the steepest increase of `f` at that point. Walk that way and you gain height fastest.
- **Magnitude**: its length tells you how steep that steepest path is. A long gradient vector means a sharp slope; a near-zero one means you're standing somewhere close to flat — a peak, valley, or saddle.

Walk the opposite direction — negative the gradient — and you descend fastest. That single fact is the entire idea behind gradient descent: nudge your position (or a model's parameters) a small step in the direction the gradient points away from, and you're moving downhill toward a lower value as efficiently as a local, one-step decision allows.

## Why this matters for a neural network

A neural network's "hillside" isn't two-dimensional — it's a loss function over every weight in the network, which can mean billions of dimensions. You can't picture that landscape, but the math doesn't care: the gradient is still just a vector, one entry per weight, and each entry answers the same question — if I nudge this one weight slightly, does the loss go up or down, and how much?

That's what makes gradients the right tool for training. Instead of guessing which of billions of weights to adjust, [backpropagation](/learn/maths-foundations/backpropagation-intuition) computes the exact gradient of the loss with respect to every weight in one efficient pass, and the training step moves each weight a little against its own gradient entry.

## A concrete two-weight example

Say a tiny model has two weights, `w1` and `w2`, and a loss function `L(w1, w2)`. If at the current values the gradient works out to:

```
∇L = (4, -1)
```

that means increasing `w1` slightly increases the loss steeply (slope 4), while increasing `w2` slightly decreases the loss gently (slope -1). A training step subtracts a small multiple of this vector from the weights — shrinking `w1`, nudging `w2` up a touch — because that's the direction of steepest descent. Repeat that thousands of times and the weights walk downhill into a low-loss region.

The gradient itself doesn't tell you how big a step to take — that's a separate choice, explored in [gradient descent, intuitively](/learn/maths-foundations/gradient-descent-intuition). It only tells you which way is downhill, and how sharply.
