A résumé for this seat is judged on two things a normal software-engineering résumé is not: whether you have real customer-facing evidence, and whether your bullets state a measured outcome instead of a job description. Get both right and the résumé does most of the work before the recruiter screen even starts.

## Cut the adjectives, keep the measurement

The single most common résumé weakness for this seat is not a missing skill. It is a bullet that describes effort instead of outcome. Compare these directly.

**Before:** "Built a robust, scalable data pipeline to improve reporting efficiency for the finance team."

**After:** "Rebuilt the finance team's monthly reconciliation pipeline; report generation went from a two-day manual process to under twenty minutes, run weekly since launch."

**Before:** "Worked closely with customers to gather requirements and deliver AI-powered solutions."

**After:** "Ran discovery interviews with three pilot customers, then shipped a document-extraction workflow against a fifteen-example eval set two of them had never had before; both renewed at the end of the pilot."

**Before:** "Passionate about leveraging cutting-edge technology to solve complex business problems."

**After:** (Delete. This line contains no information. Replace it with a bullet describing one thing you actually shipped.)

**Before:** "Led a cross-functional team to deploy a machine learning model into production."

**After:** "Deployed a support-ticket routing model into the customer's existing Zendesk instance; I owned the model and the eval, a teammate owned the front-end queue view. Average time-to-first-response fell from roughly four hours to under twenty minutes over the following two weeks."

**Before:** "Experienced in Python, SQL, Docker, and cloud infrastructure."

**After:** (Skills lists still belong on a résumé, but as a labelled section, not as a bullet pretending to be an accomplishment. Move it below the experience section, not above it.)

Notice what changed in each rewrite: a specific number, a stated before-and-after, and, where it applies, a named customer-facing action, an interview run, a pilot, a renewal. None of the "after" versions claim more than actually happened. Vin Vashishta's standard for a strong FDE candidate, "outcomes from discovery through adoption," applies exactly as much to a résumé bullet as it does to a full portfolio write-up.

## The line most SWE résumés are missing

Pragmatic Engineer's interview with three FDE leads names the specific thing that separates an FDE from a standard software engineer on paper: comfort with ambiguity and genuine customer empathy, demonstrated, not claimed. A résumé that lists only internal engineering work, however strong, is missing the evidence a hiring manager for this seat is scanning for first. If you have any customer-facing history at all, an internal stakeholder you presented to regularly, a support escalation you personally resolved, a pilot you ran, put it on the résumé explicitly, even if it was a small part of a larger role. The ElevenLabs candidate's account of their own recruiter screen makes the same point from the other side: the very first thing that loop pressed on was specificity about individual ownership, and a résumé that already answers that question saves the recruiter screen from having to dig for it.

If you genuinely have no customer-facing history yet, do not invent one. Name the closest real thing you have, a demo you gave to non-technical stakeholders, a bootcamp or capstone from this path where you played both engineer and discovery lead, and be explicit that it is simulated where it is. A labelled simulation is credible. An implied real deployment that was not one is not.

## Keep the register honest

No self-congratulation, and no over-hedging either. "Rebuilt the reconciliation pipeline; report generation went from two days to twenty minutes" does not need "successfully" or "significantly" in front of it; the number is doing the work. It also does not need to be softened into "helped improve reporting somewhat," which throws away the exact evidence a reader is looking for. State the number plainly and move on to the next line.

## LinkedIn: the headline and the About section

The headline is prime real estate and most people waste it on a job title alone. "Forward Deployed Engineer at X" tells a recruiter nothing they cannot already see. A stronger pattern: "Forward Deployed Engineer, shipped [specific thing] into [specific kind of environment]", for example, "Forward Deployed Engineer, evals and deployment for customer-facing LLM systems." Name the work, not just the title.

The About section should open with two sentences that answer, immediately, what you actually do and for whom, using the same measured-outcome discipline as a résumé bullet, not a summary of your personality. A weak opener: "I am a driven engineer passionate about solving hard problems at the intersection of AI and business." A stronger one: "I build and deploy customer-facing AI systems, most recently a document-extraction workflow evaluated against a fifteen-example expert-labelled set, shipped into a customer's existing ticketing system." The second version is longer by a few words and worth every one of them, because it is checkable and specific in exactly the way the first is not.

## What to leave off

Both surfaces are improved as much by deletion as by rewriting. Cut buzzwords that carry no information on their own: "synergy," "cutting-edge," "10x," "rockstar," and any self-applied label like "AI expert" that a reader has no way to verify. Cut duplicate bullets that restate the same accomplishment in a résumé line and a LinkedIn post; a recruiter who reads both should learn something new from the second one, not see the first one paraphrased. And resist the pull to list every framework, model provider, and tool you have ever touched in a skills section; a shorter, accurate list reads as more credible than a long one padded with things you used once, because an interviewer who tests you against an inflated list will notice the gap immediately, and that gap costs more trust than the longer list ever bought you.

The through-line for both surfaces is the same one this path has argued from its first page: what you can prove beats what you claim, every time, and a résumé or profile that only ever claims is easy to tell apart from one that shows its work.
