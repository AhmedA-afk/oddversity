A student turns in an essay. An employee sends a client a report. Neither mentions that an AI helped write part of it — and if either one turns out to be wrong, plagiarized, or embarrassing, the AI doesn't answer for it. They do.

## What it is

"Using AI responsibly" gets treated like one vague virtue. It's actually three separate obligations, and it helps to keep them apart because each calls for a different action:

1. **Disclosure** — telling the people relying on your work whether and how AI was involved, when that fact would change how they read it, trust it, grade it, or pay for it.
2. **Not borrowing authority you don't have** — not presenting AI-generated analysis, citations, or opinions as if they were your own expert judgment or independently verified fact, when they aren't.
3. **Owning the output** — whatever goes out with your name on it is your responsibility, regardless of what produced the words. "The AI said so" is not a defense to your professor, your boss, your reader, or your client.

None of this is about whether AI is good or bad to reach for — that's a separate question, covered in [what AI can and can't do](/learn/ai-literacy/what-ai-can-and-cant-do-overview). This is about what happens *after* you've used it: what you tell people, and what you're still on the hook for.

## The mental model

Picture AI the way you'd picture a research assistant or ghostwriter you hired for an afternoon. Hiring one isn't shameful and doesn't need a confession at the top of every document. But two things stay true no matter how good the assistant is:

- **The signature at the bottom is still yours.** An author who hires a ghostwriter still owns every factual claim under their byline. You don't outsource the responsibility along with the labor.
- **Whether you mention the assistant depends on what the reader is trying to judge.** A dinner party doesn't need to know who catered it. A restaurant review does, because the reviewer's own judgment is the entire point of the write-up.

That second point is the real test: **is the person relying on this trying to judge something AI use would change their read on?** A teacher assessing whether *you* can structure an argument needs to know if AI did the structuring. A client who just wants a correct summary by Friday mostly needs the summary to be correct — though if the organization has a stated policy, or the client would clearly want to know, that overrides "mostly."

This is a different question from [judging whether the output is true](/learn/ai-literacy/the-single-most-important-skill-judging-output). That's about the content. This is about whether the recipient has an accurate picture of how it was made.

## Why it works this way

Trust systems — schools, employers, journalism, professional licensing — run on an assumption: the claims in front of you trace back to a verified source, whether that's a credentialed expert, a cited study, or the specific effort of the person whose name is attached. AI output can sound exactly as confident as verified work while tracing back to nothing more than a pattern in training data — that's the mechanism behind [why AI sounds so confident](/learn/ai-literacy/why-ai-sounds-so-confident) even when it's wrong. Disclosure exists to keep that trust assumption accurate. It isn't saying "AI-assisted work is worse." It's saying "here's what this actually traces back to — judge accordingly."

That's also why disclosure norms differ by context instead of being one universal rule. A classroom is testing whether *you* built a skill. A newsroom is vouching for a claim's accuracy to the public. A workplace mostly cares whether the deliverable is correct and client data stayed safe. Different things are being verified, so different things need disclosing — and the norms are genuinely still settling, which is why policies vary so much between one school and the next, or one company and the next.

Deception and impersonation sit in a different category entirely, and it's worth not blurring them with disclosure norms. Deciding how much detail to put in an acknowledgment is a judgment call reasonable people disagree on. Generating a fake quote and attributing it to a real person, cloning someone's voice without consent, or inventing a fake reviewer isn't a milder version of that judgment call — it's using the tool to make someone believe something false about who said or did what. No amount of "the norms are still settling" covers that; it was never a gray area.

## A concrete example

**The student.** She's writing a history essay. She asks an AI to explain a concept she's stuck on, then separately asks it to critique her draft's structure. She writes every sentence herself.

Her process:
- Check the syllabus, or ask directly — many courses now state an AI policy per assignment. If one exists, it overrides her own judgment.
- If nothing is stated, ask what the assignment is actually measuring. It's measuring her ability to research, structure, and argue — not whether she has access to a study partner. Using AI as a study partner and disclosing that is a very different claim from having it draft the argument and disclosing that.
- She adds one line to her submission: *"I used AI to clarify [the concept] and get feedback on my draft's structure; the writing and argument are my own."* Honest, specific, no overclaiming and nothing hidden.
- Separately, she checks every date and fact the AI gave her before it goes in the essay — a citation-shaped sentence isn't the same thing as a checked one.

**The employee.** He uses AI to draft the first pass of a client status report, pasting in some project notes to speed it up.

His process:
- Before pasting anything, he checks whether those notes contain client-confidential information — that's a [data-handling question](/learn/ai-literacy/what-not-to-paste-into-ai) that comes *before* the disclosure question, not after.
- He checks his company's AI policy the same way the student checked her syllabus. Plenty of workplaces now require disclosure on client-facing work, or restrict which tools can touch which data — see [data privacy, provenance, and policy](/learn/ai-literacy/data-privacy-provenance-and-policy).
- He rewrites and fact-checks the draft against the real project numbers before sending it — not as a courtesy, but because his name is what the client will hold accountable if a number is wrong. "AI drafted it" will not land well in that meeting.
- Whether the *client* needs telling that AI was involved depends on his company's stance and industry norms. Whether *he* needs to personally verify every claim before it goes out is not optional in any version of this.

Notice what's constant across both: neither needed to feel guilty about using the tool. Both needed to know their institution's stated policy, think about what disclosure would actually tell the reader, and verify the content before putting their name on it.

## Where it shows up

- **School** — AI policies increasingly live per-assignment, not per-course; a brainstorming pass and a fully generated essay can fall under completely different rules in the same class.
- **Work** — internal notes, client deliverables, and code all raise the same question at different stakes: a Slack summary is low-stakes, a signed audit or legal filing is not, and the disclosure-and-verification bar should track that difference.
- **Journalism and publishing** — outlets have taken real reputational damage from AI-generated text or fabricated bylined "experts" published without disclosure; most serious newsrooms now have explicit written policies for exactly this reason.
- **Creative work and portfolios** — competitions and client commissions increasingly ask directly whether AI was used in generation; answering honestly is standard practice now, not an exceptional confession.
- **Anything with a "who actually said this" question attached** — reviews, testimonials, quotes, recommendation letters. If a human's name or voice is attached to a claim, that claim needs to have actually come from that human.

## Watch out for

- **Treating "no explicit policy" as "no obligation."** Most institutions haven't caught up with written rules yet. Absence of a policy isn't permission — it just means you're the one reasoning about what the reader would want to know, using the test from the mental model above.
- **Using disclosure as a substitute for verification.** Saying "AI helped with this" doesn't make an unchecked, possibly invented fact okay to publish — see [what a hallucination really is](/learn/ai-literacy/what-a-hallucination-really-is). Disclosure and verification are separate obligations; doing one doesn't excuse skipping the other.
- **Blurring disclosure norms with the deception line.** Deciding how much detail belongs in an acknowledgment is a judgment call. Faking a quote, cloning a voice, or inventing a credentialed source isn't a milder version of that call — it's a different category of problem, and "everyone's still figuring out AI norms" doesn't cover it.

## Where next

The thread running through school, work, and everywhere else is the same: disclosure protects the reader's trust, verification protects the truth, and your name covers both. If you only keep one habit from this, make it: before anything goes out under your name, ask what happens if it's wrong — then check accordingly.
